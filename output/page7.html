Just in time for Halloween, HBO has revealed its casting for some key supporting characters in its Harry Potter TV adaptation. The show has already revealed who will be its main cast, so now it's time to see which actors will add some undead color to Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry.

Shameless and Fargo actor William H. Phantom Blade Zero

Wesley LeBlanc

Phantom Blade Zero is one of the best action games I’ve ever played, and it’s certainly one of the most impressive to watch. Beijing-based developer S-Game uses China’s Wuxia brand of action (which was actually banned for some time during the 20th Century by the country’s government) to create a stylish, fluid, and hyper-engaging combat system. It will constantly leave you in awe that action combat can look this good. But looks aren’t everything. Fortunately, Phantom Blade Zero delivers on all fronts, with weapon mechanics so detailed that picking up a new blade can feel like unlocking a new character in a fighting game. The story won’t blow your socks off, but it’s certainly engaging and enticing enough to bring you through Phantom Blade Zero’s 40 or so hours of content. If you’re not interested in the “perfect ending,” which is the only satisfactory one in my opinion, you can complete the game in less than 30 hours. S-Game mixes high-octane combat with sprawling and rewarding exploration to create a must-play during this already busy season of games. You can read more of my thoughts in the Game Informer Phantom Blade Zero review, and if you are hopping in this weekend, be sure to check out my list of Phantom Blade Zero tips and tricks I wish I knew before starting it.

Halloween: The Game

Wesley LeBlanc

If you have any fond memories of IllFonic’s ill-fated Friday the 13th: The Game as I do, you simply must play the studio’s latest, Halloween: The Game. It is essentially Friday the 13th with Michael Myers instead of Jason, but with a decade of lessons learned from the team’s teeth carving in other horror properties and games. You’re either Michael or a citizen; the former wants to kill you and all the NPCs in various suburban maps, while the latter must rescue as many NPCs as possible while staying alive themselves. It’s that classic cat-and-mouse formula IllFonic is the master of, but with all the style, substance, and synths of John Carpenter’s Halloween masterpiece. Plus, Halloween: The Game features IllFonic’s first crack at a single-player campaign, and while it’s largely a tutorial for playing as Michael, if you’re a Halloween mega-fan like me, it’s worth checking out if only to take part in some of the film’s most iconic kills. You can read more of my thoughts in the Game Informer Halloween: The Game review here, and if you’re hopping in this weekend, there’s a new map, additional Halloween-themed cosmetics to unlock, and more waiting for you.

Overwatch Halloween Event

Kyle Hilliard

Overwatch is in the midst of its Halloween event, which have always historically been pretty interesting. This year it's Mystery Madness: Graveyard Games. In it, you begin by picking one of three characters in a random role. And then, as the game progresses, you choose perks that upgrade, alter, or completely change what your character can do. It can get really chaotic where by round three characters like Orisa might be three times her normal size or Torbjorn might have placed a giant turret on Mercy's making her a flying death machine. There are also Halloween skins, as well.

Tom The Postgirl

Charles Harte

Tom the Postgirl is an odd little adventure that I'd recommend to fans of Untitled Goose Game. You play as Tom, a creepy girl who's tasked with delivering packages to the people of her wacky town, which is depicted with storybook-style illustrations and a color palette limited to black, white, and red. On the surface, you're delivering packages, but the game's real story is based on Tom's tendency to peep where she shouldn't, peering into townsfolks' windows to watch their odd lives. On house is full of kids who live like dogs; another has a couple with a bizarre obsession with tickling. You never know what each window might reveal, and I quickly sympathized with (and enabled) Tom's compulsion. You also have the capacity to cause quite a bit of trouble by stomping on flowers, haphazardly snipping strings and cords, or even stealing packages and using them for your own mischievous purposes. It's a pretty short experience, completable in just a few hours, making it a nice palate cleanser between the massive releases at the end of the year.

Virtue And A Sledgehammer

Marcus Stewart

This Devolver Digital-published game has a simple but head-turning premise. You’re a profoundly angry woman who returns to her small hometown armed with nothing but a sledgehammer, which she uses to smash everything in sight. The town is also inhabited by robots possessing human consciousnesses. Swinging the hammer can either punch holes through walls or, with enough force, reduce entire buildings to rubble. But why is she doing this? Why is she so mad? A big part of Virtue and a Sledgehammer’s allure is its fascinating and well-written tale about the protagonist’s strained relationships with her sister and mother. Learning more about their lives requires you to keep smashing, but the more you learn, the more you begin to question whether smashing everything is actually a good idea.

Titanium Court On Switch 2

Marcus Stewart

This bizarre indie blends fun match-3 puzzle-solving with tower defense gameplay wrapped around a funny but surreal story. The match-3 gameplay involves sliding tiles to match geographic elements like castles, trees, and rivers to collect resources that feed into the tower defense portion. Depending on how the board looks when the puzzle portion ends, you send troops (sustained by the resources you collected) to attack/defend against rival castles and gather more items; board positioning determines how well this can go. The two mechanics feed into each other to create a fun loop that grows in complexity as new mechanics are introduced. Did I mention the game is also very weird? The silly tale centers on a medieval court designating you the chosen savior to rescue their lost queen (and many other tasks), and the dumb tone adds personality to a sometimes dry genre. Titanium Court charmed and impressed us when it launched on PC earlier this year, and now console and mobile players get their chance to hold court.

Giant Squid also made 2016's Abzû and 2020's The Pathless. It's a talented team and Sword of the Sea maintains that reputation. And now you can play it on Switch 2. Matt Miller

Curse of Resthaven: Tips and Tricks to Unravel the Island’s Secrets

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Hi there! I’m Scott Christian, the studio director of Hilltop Studios here in Toronto, and the game director of Curse of Resthaven. We make games with a particular emphasis on strong characters, intricate storytelling, unique mechanics, and the occasional musical interlude.

Before making games, I spent fifteen years working as a composer and conductor here in Toronto, mainly in theatre. That background informs how we approach our games at Hilltop: character, timing, and a good dramatic reveal go a long way. Our debut, Lil’ Guardsman, was a comedic fantasy gate-guard simulator about a twelve-year-old put in charge of a castle gate, with the fate of the world in her hands.

For our follow-up, Curse of Resthaven, we decided to take a different turn into gothic horror, creeping dread, and a mystery that unfolds over a repeating six-day time loop. You play as Ambrose, the island’s newly appointed governor, tasked with restoring prosperity to the colony but he also has his own agenda: finding his missing niece, Amalia. The problem is that Resthaven is destroyed six days into his tenure… and Ambrose is sent back to the day of his arrival, the only one to remember the island’s fate.

Mashing up mechanics from narrative adventures, trading sims, deckbuilders, and roguelikes, Curse of Resthaven challenges you to investigate a town full of secrets, keep its economy running, and venture into the strange wilderness beyond its borders. You have six days to work with before disaster strikes and the island resets. Fortunately, you get to keep what you learn.

Solving the mystery is just the first step. You’ll soon discover there are three different timelines to explore, and only by reaching all three conclusions will you unlock the fourth, true ending.

Whether you’re stepping onto Resthaven for the first time or looking to make the most of your next loop, here are a few tips to help you get started.

A Few Simple Tips

Curse of Resthaven is a game about connecting the dots. Its systems feed into one another, and the best discoveries often come from combining information gathered in different ways. Here are a few things to keep in mind as you investigate the island.

  • Talk to everyone, and then talk to them again. Resthaven’s residents have their own motivations, grudges, and secrets. What someone tells you depends on what Ambrose already knows, what topics you bring up, and what has happened during the current loop. If a character isn’t being helpful, you may simply need to learn a little more before approaching them. Your journal can be counted on to let you know when to ask someone about something again.
  • Pay attention during interrogations. You can question characters about different topics to uncover clues, advance quests, and learn more about the island. Information gathered from one resident may unlock the topic needed to move the needle with another. If you’re stuck, revisit people you’ve spoken to and see whether they have a perspective on newly acquired topics or, better yet, can guide you in the right direction.
  • Don’t treat trading as a side activity. As governor, Ambrose is responsible for keeping Resthaven’s economy moving. Merchant ships bring goods to buy and sell, while the island’s businesses can be upgraded to produce useful resources. Money is only part of the equation: having the right resources is essential for pursuing opportunities elsewhere, including expeditions into the wilderness.
  • Experiment with your approach. Not every problem has an obvious solution, and not every promising lead will pay off immediately. Try different approaches, pursue unexpected conversations, and see where your investigations take you. Even a botched loop may teach you something useful for the next one.

Make the Most of the Time Loop

The six-day time loop is at the heart of Curse of Resthaven. Each loop begins with the island’s inhabitants returning to their usual routines, but Ambrose retains his knowledge and progression. That distinction is everything.

You aren’t expected to solve the entire mystery in one pass. Instead, each loop gives you another opportunity to apply what you’ve learned, approach old problems differently, and uncover information that was previously out of reach.

  • Knowledge is your most valuable resource. A character who refuses to cooperate on Day 1 might be much more forthcoming once you’ve discovered what motivates them. An apparently impossible obstacle might become straightforward once you know the right person to talk to or the right question to ask.
  • Plan your days around what you want to learn. There are more people to question, businesses to manage, and places to explore than you can necessarily get to in a single loop. Focus on the leads that seem most promising, and don’t worry if you have to leave something for next time.
  • Use each reset as an opportunity. When the island resets, you can take a different approach with the benefit of everything you’ve discovered. You don’t have to repeat the same actions just because you did them last time. If you already know where a particular lead is going, spend that time pursuing something new.

The goal isn’t simply to repeat the same six days until you get everything right. It’s to use what you learned last time to change what happens the next time.

Venture Beyond the Town

Beyond the safety of Resthaven’s fortified walls lies a much stranger version of the island. Expeditions take Ambrose into this wilderness through a card-based narrative system that blends roguelikes, deckbuilders, and choose-your-own-adventure games.

Each expedition draws encounters from a pool, meaning your journey can unfold differently from one attempt to the next. You’ll face decisions, discover strange events, and encounter opportunities that may not appear again on your next trip.

  • Expect the unexpected. Expeditions aren’t fixed sequences of events. The encounters you discover on one outing may be very different from those on another, so stay curious and be prepared to adapt.
  • Don’t assume you’ve seen everything. There are more encounters than you’ll experience in a single run. Returning to the wilderness on subsequent playthroughs can reveal entirely new stories and discoveries.
  • Remember that expeditions are part of the larger investigation. The wilderness isn’t separate from the mystery unfolding in town. Information and resources you gather can help you make progress elsewhere, so consider how an expedition might support your other goals.

Explore Every Corner Of Resthaven

It’s worth taking the time to look around. Beyond the main investigation, Resthaven contains optional discoveries, including collectible moth relics and other secrets tucked away throughout the town.

These discoveries reward players who aren’t in a rush. You can barely take a single step if you rely heavily on the handy town map + fast travel functionality, but you’ll certainly miss out on some optional, flavourful side content. Give yourself permission to wander a little. After all, in Resthaven you have nothing but time (in six-day intervals)!

And don’t be afraid to revisit familiar places. As you learn more about the colony and its inhabitants, locations and conversations that seemed unremarkable at first may take on new significance.

What To Do If You’re Stuck

Your journal is your best friend. Check your active leads for hints about what to pursue next. If you’ve exhausted all the possibilities in town, it may be time to head back out on expeditions and look for Amalia’s campsites. And your journal will probably tell you so!

Your journal has another trick up its sleeve, too. After the opening hours, you’ll unlock a timeline showing important events across the six-day loop and where they take place. If you’re stumped by a “???” on the timeline, it means something important is happening at that moment you haven’t discovered yet. To progress, you might just need to be in the right place at the right time.

The town map is another easy-to-miss resource. It shows which characters are accessible in each zone at any given time. Once you’ve spoken to a named NPC, their question mark becomes a portrait, making it much easier to track them down in subsequent loops. Still seeing a “?” in a zone? That might be a good place to start looking for someone new.

Finally, don’t underestimate the power of a little community gossip. Some leads require interrogation, but the answer might simply be to ask the townspeople about a particular topic. Resthaven is a small, tight-knit community, and information has a way of getting around.

We’ve designed the investigation around a trail of clues(We’ve classed them as  bronze/silver/gold behind the scenes, but you won’t see these terms in-game.)

  • Bronze clues give you a little information and nudge you in the right direction, and many townspeople have them
  • Silver clues offer more specific details and bring you closer to the truth; fewer townspeople have them
  • Gold clues unambiguously reveal the next step in a lead. Only one or two townspeople have them

Follow the trail, and you shouldn’t be left out in the cold for long.

Don’t Worry About Seeing Everything the First Time

Curse of Resthaven features three branching timelines, each revealing different pieces of the larger mystery. You won’t necessarily encounter every quest or revelation during a single playthrough, and that’s intentional.

As you progress, you’ll uncover new information that changes how you understand the island and its people. Some discoveries will answer questions, while others will leave you with even more to investigate.

Once you’ve completed a timeline, New Game+ introduces Ambrose’s growing sense of déjà vu, allowing you to fast-track through content you’ve already experienced and focus on what’s different. You can spend less time retracing familiar steps and more time exploring new possibilities.

So don’t worry about finding every secret on your first visit. Pay attention, follow the leads that interest you, and let your growing knowledge guide you through the next loop.

Resthaven has a great many secrets. The trick is figuring out which ones to pursue, when to pursue them, and what to do with what you discover.

Six days remain. Good luck, Governor. You’ll need it.

EA Just Shelved A Mysterious Sims Project—That Could Be Good News For Players

It's been another rough year for video games, with countless studio closures, staff layoffs, and projects that barely managed to even get off the ground before they were unceremoniously cut short. But things are feeling especially grim after EA trotted out two beloved franchises--Mass Effect and Dragon Age--for what amounted to a public execution via the announcement that BioWare has been shuttered. 

This was especially painful for Mass Effect players, who were looking forward to the upcoming Mass Effect 5, which we now know was tentatively titled Mass Effect: Universe. But it's not just BioWare fans who are upset at the moment. Fans of EA's other properties are growing increasingly concerned about the future of those franchises, if any future is planned for them at all. My colleague Levi Winslow recently wrote about their concerns for the Skate franchise in the wake of layoffs at its development studio. Meanwhile, I'm quite worried about the fate of The Sims.

In the same breath it announced the end of BioWare, EA also reaffirmed its commitment to Apex Legends, Skate, and The Sims franchises. But everyone knows that when it comes to profits, EA's sports titles outrun its other IPs by a country mile. If EA is willing to pull the plug on legacy franchises like Mass Effect and Dragon Age, where does that leave its other franchises, like The Sims 4?

EA is pivoting its focus from Project Rene to Project Evergreen.

According to EA, Sims fans need not worry, as the company is committed to "focusing on the long-term potential of The Sims." Apparently, focusing on that long-term potential involves shelving a project The Sims team has been working on for years: Project Rene, AKA Project Lotus, AKA the nebulous next entry in The Sims franchise, which appears to be some kind of weird Sims MMO mobile game thing. Instead, EA will be focusing on Project Evergreen, which is rumored to be a sort of remaster of The Sims 4, with all (or most) DLC packs included, and recurring bugs squashed.

"We're supporting this franchise by pausing work on one of the next titles in this franchise" doesn't exactly inspire confidence, especially after the nonchalance with which EA just killed two other beloved game franchises. But I think this actually might be the right move. 

It's been a bumpy couple of years for The Sims, and many players aren't particularly enthused about Project Rene/Lotus, as it seems to be once again trying to recreate The Sims Online, something EA has tried to rebuild over and over. In fact, much of the reason The Sims 4 launched in such awful shape was due to the fact that it was initially meant to be an MMO-style game that could run on mobile devices. Less than a year before launch, EA pivoted to a more traditional Sims game--but by then, so much of the game had been built with online play and mobile devices in mind that it was too late to turn back. Despite releasing over 100 DLC packs and embarking on a concentrated campaign to squash the game's many, many, many bugs, longtime Sims players are still frustrated with the state of The Sims 4, often pointing out the numerous ways it pales in comparison to its predecessor, The Sims 3. 

The Sims 4 is officially "done" in that there are no plans to release any more expansion packs for the game. When it comes to the future of the franchise, most players don't want the next Sims title to be yet another attempt at shoehorning online and/or mobile gameplay into a game that is, at its core, all about building and playing with digital dolls in their digital dollhouses. So EA's decision to hit pause on Project Rene/Lotus's development isn't something I find especially heartbreaking. In fact, I think it's the first truly wise decision EA's made regarding the franchise in a long time, provided EA actually plans to support Project Evergreen long-term (which the name Evergreen would suggest).

Sims 4 players don't want Project Rene, but they do want either The Sims 5 or a complete, playable version of The Sims 4--ideally one that doesn't require the purchase of $1,600 in DLC packs to feel "finished." Hitting pause on its weird, experimental Sims projects in favor of attempting to preserve The Sims 4 in a stable, playable, "evergreen" state is arguably the best decision EA could make, given the current state of both EA and The Sims franchise. Pouring resources into a concept that has failed before (online MMO-style Sims gameplay) rather than a tried-and-true formula (a digital dollhouse) would only harm the franchise at this point.

Whatever Project Rene is supposed to be, it's officially on ice for now.

Of course, there's no telling what kind of shape Project Evergreen will launch in, assuming it does successfully make it to launch. It sounds like the Evergreen devs have a deadline to hit--hence the reshuffling of resources away from Rene/Lotus--but even with the increase in development resources, there's no guarantee Evergreen will be a massive hit with players.

Provided the rest of development does go smoothly and Evergreen is indeed well-received by players, it may still fall short of EA's goals when it comes to profits. EA has spent the last decade doing everything possible to monetize the living hell out of The Sims 4, a game that--unlike EA's lucrative sports catalog--is largely intended to be a solitary gaming experience that doesn't require online play. Back in the olden days of The Sims 1, 2, and 3, players would purchase the base game, a few expansion packs, and maybe a couple of "stuff packs" for extra decor options, but that was all one really needed to enjoy the game. 

The Sims 4, however, introduced an era of seemingly endless DLC: expansion packs, game packs, stuff packs, kits--there are over 100 of them, ranging in cost from $40 to $5, and totaling around $1,600 if purchased individually outside of sales and bundles. In addition to the constant stream of DLC releases, EA has in recent years implemented daily login rewards, various events and challenges, and even a paid mod store nobody asked for--all in the hopes of getting players to spend more time and money on The Sims 4. For a while there, I half-expected EA to implement a Sims 4 battle pass.

EA trimming down its Sims projects is fine with me, as long as the series isn't uprooted entirely.

There's no telling what sort of irritating monetization tactics we'll see in Project Evergreen, but at the very least, Evergreen sounds like an attempt to try and maintain the series' roots, rather than continue chasing the mobile MMO Roblox-like direction Project Lotus seemed to be taking. Even better, Evergreen may turn out to be the game Sims players have been aching for since The Sims 4 launched to middling reviews back in 2014: a game that isn't fundamentally broken and lacking in content.

The real question is if Evergreen will be successful enough to keep The Sims franchise alive. Given that EA's new owners were happy to kill off what was shaping up to be a potentially great Mass Effect game, it's not hard to imagine a world where the Grim Reaper inevitably comes for The Sims, regardless of whether players love or hate Project Evergreen. After all, feelings don't generate dollar signs, and EA's new owners have made one thing abundantly clear: killing off an iconic franchise is "worth it" if it saves EA a few Simoleons.

CoD: Modern Warfare To Cut Real-Life Korean Tragedy Segment After Backlash

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Publisher Activision has responded to criticism over using a devastating tragedy in Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4, saying that the incident will be removed from the military shooter in a November 12 update.

The Seoul Halloween crowd crush occurred in October 2022 in Itaewon. Approximately 100,000 people gathered in a narrow alleyway in Seoul, resulting in 160 casualties as people were suffocated. This year marks the fourth anniversary of the tragedy, with bereaved families and community organizers gathering to remember the lives that were lost.

Modern Warfare 4 hit consoles and PC on October 23, and not long after its release, the game drew criticism from bereaved Korean families. In the opening cutscene to the game's campaign--which focuses on a fictional war between North and South Korea--Modern Warfare 4 depicts a chaotic scene where one person performs CPR on a downed individual, while bodies are either laid out on the streets or carried away on stretchers. Given the game's setting and the events on screen, the 10.29 Itaewon Disaster Bereaved Families Association and the Citizens' Countermeasures Meeting--two organizations formed after the 2022 tragedy in Seoul--were "appalled" at the calamity's inclusion and demanded that Activision remove it from the game.

"Using such an incident as material for a game intended for entertainment purposes is itself highly inappropriate and a senseless act," the groups said, according to the BBC. "The Itaewon tragedy was a tragic disaster that produced hundreds of casualties, and citizens who participated in the rescue efforts or witnessed the event at the time continue to suffer from trauma. If the game developer has even a minimum level of courtesy and empathy toward the victims, they should promptly take corrective measures."

Activision has heard the call and plans to remove the depiction from Modern Warfare 4 when an update arrives on November 12. In responding to the criticism, Activision said it never intended to trivialize the disaster or make light of human suffering.

"In developing Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4, we approached the subject matter with care and respect. The game’s opening cinematic utilises some real-world news coverage to establish the game’s fictional narrative," Activision said. "It is not intended to reference or trivialise the Itaewon disaster or any other real-world tragedy in any way, shape or form."

Ramayana: Rise of a Legend Review

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ramayana: Rise of a Legend is in theaters beginning Nov. 5.

Hindi cinema has long looked to Hollywood as a benchmark for big-screen action; much of its 21st century style, for instance, was inspired by Mission: Impossible 2. Ramayana: Rise of a Legend re-imagines the resplendent Hindu epic as a CGI-laden blockbuster at a time when the Marvel Cinematic Universe has become the de facto touchstone for big-budget adaptations. In that vein, Nitesh Tiwari’s undertaking does, in fact, resemble a Marvel movie. Unfortunately, that Marvel movie happens to be Thor: Love and Thunder, a much-derided film whose floating head VFX had to be famously reworked.

This resemblance takes hold in an early scene, in which the epic’s antagonist, Ravana (charismatic Toxic star Yash), prays atop a cliff to the creator-god Brahma, whose countenance takes the form of a disembodied constellation with an overly-expressive digital mouth. This excess fluidity is owed, in part, to the movie’s uncanny English dub, which was shown to press in the United States in lieu of the original Hindi version (which happens to be 18 minutes longer than this 162-minute cut). Rather than traditional dubbing, the filmmakers developed their own in-house Generative A.I. technology (fittingly called Brahma A.I.) to sync up the characters’ lips to the flowery English dialogue. Some are calling it the future of cinema, but in its current incarnation, the result resembles exaggerated mouth movements superimposed on still images for comedy sketches, à la The Annoying Orange.

This effect is befitting of such a robotic adaptation, one which briefly threatens to interrogate the several millennia-old text of the title (in the vein of recent Thai drama The Burning Giants, which critiques the violence at the heart of national myths) before locking itself into a didactic approach. It’s a rigid depiction of both the subcontinent-spanning plot, as well as the theme of “Dharma,” or sacred duty, which the characters verbalize ad nauseum and follow unconditionally, and uninspiringly. For the uninitiated — which is to say, Sony’s target audience for the English dub, whose Indian subtitle (“Part 1”) has been slyly replaced with “Rise of a Legend” to convince people they’re paying for a complete story — the plot concerns an ongoing war between demigods the Devas and demons the Asuras.

The film re-imagines the villainous Ravana as a kind of liberator gone mad. The Asura leader begins by praying for the power to free his enslaved clan, and his island kingdom of Lanka, from Deva oppression before conquering nearby cities through violent conquest with his ambitious siblings by his side.. This forces the demigods to turn to the protector deity Vishnu, who re-incarnates himself as the human-born Rama (Ranbir Kapoor), one of four princes of the kingdom of Ayodhya. Prologues pile up on prologues for nearly an hour, as Rama grows up learning the ways of war and wisdom, since his divine destiny is to defeat the Asuras for good.

The filmmakers’ decision to cleave the story in half leaves it hanging in suspended animation.

However, the filmmakers’ decision to cleave the story in half leaves it hanging in suspended animation. It ends where the central plot of the Ramayana — Rama’s journey to rescuing his devoted wife Sita (Sai Pallavi) from Ravana’s clutches — finally kicks off in earnest, yielding an episodic experience where the central hero and villain are, at best, vaguely aware of one another’s existence. They never actually come into contact, leaving Ramayana: Rise of a Legend with the responsibility of establishing a protagonist and antagonist alluring enough that viewers with no pre-existing religious or cultural attachment will feel dutybound to return in one year’s time — or whenever the sequel is ready.

How does this work out? As far as Ravana is concerned, Kannada-language superstar Yash makes a meal of the character, imagined as an action anti-hero who dances in fiery worship of the destroyer god Shiva, and who nails his enemies with jackknife powerbombs, as vengeful gears turn slowly in his mind. Traditionally depicted with 10 heads side by side, the movie’s version of Ravana re-imagines this as a kind of fissure of personality, wherein 10 different versions of the demon king (some old, some young; some confident, some sniveling) converse and argue within his consciousness. He’s ruthless in one moment, but respectful in the next, making for the closest thing the movie has to complexity before it returns to the story’s binary good-versus-evil traditions. Add to this the fact that the movie’s Lanka is vibrant, energetic and sensual, compared to its stilted, austere Ayodhya, and it becomes hard to root for the ostensible “good guys.”

Unfortunately, this becomes doubly true whenever Rama is on screen. The film’s depiction of him is an utter bore. An embodiment of virtue in the abstract, the heroic prince — who agrees to live in exile for 14 years so one of his brothers can take the throne — is spoken of as a kind and capable figure, but his people’s worship of him is a default setting, rather than something earned through benevolence. After all, Rama is a central figure in Hinduism, so more than enough viewers will bring their own baggage to the movie. But as a story, it’s as rote as can be, a flaw that infects its dramatic DNA: Rama’s relationship to Sita. While Pallavi is afforded a tad more action and autonomy than most actresses in the role, the promise of a sword-wielding Sita is often cut short, returning her quickly to a two-dimensional damsel who shares little chemistry with her husband. Much like Kapoor’s Rama, Pallavi’s Sita is graceful in posture, but there’s little underlying either of them as actual human beings.

Rama, who lacks anything resembling outward opinions or an inner life beyond his Dharma, acquires weapons and knowledge not through practice or learning, but through gurus psychically unlocking these hidden abilities, as though the film were operating on video game logic. Each new chapter sees him “leveling up” as he fights a different, malformed CGI golem in a new forest setting, though at least he deserves credit for using his unlimited magical arrows to get these eyesores off our screens.

Saddled with clunky English dialogue, Kapoor is the perfect actor for this version of Rama, but that’s not the compliment it seems. His po-faced approach snaps perfectly into place, alongside a dramatically dull incarnation of the mythic hero, whose duty toward violent reprisal, and whose adoration by his legions of fans, is a given. Kapoor, a fourth generation Bollywood star, enjoys exactly such a quasi-religious fandom, even if the Hindu nationalist factions the film has been courting with its marketing (which refers to these myths as “history”) has since turned on him for eating meat — a religious faux pas among Hindutva extremists.

Then again, with the movie’s attempts to stoke religious fervor being all but cancelled out, perhaps it makes sense to approach Ramayana: Rise of a Legend as a hermetically sealed experience, if only for a moment. This doesn’t do it any favors either; aesthetically, it’s a headache. While the grandeur of the costumes and archaic sets might draw one’s gaze in static moments, the cumulative effect of its many action scenes is often bewildering. Sure, some individual shots are big and bold in composition, but they’re frenetic and unfocused when strung together, with points of interest shifting in and out, left and right, and up and down at random. The fantasy fights lack the necessary physical and emotional weight to make a lasting impact, even before Rama’s fellow exiled brother Lakshmana (Ravi Dubey) delivers his umpteenth comic quip met by awkward dead air, adding to the Marvel-ness of it all.

Hans Zimmer and A.R. Rahman join hands for some booming compositions, but the music — much of which has been trimmed for the shorter cut, which features little singing or dancing — is tasked with scoring scenes and set pieces with no rhythm of their own. The film feels entirely off-kilter even before characters open their distracting, digitally-rendered mouths. That some of them sound like whiny, posh South Bombay youngsters is, perhaps, something only viewers who’ve spent time in India might really notice in the English version, but it nonetheless robs the dialogue-heavy drama of its potential grandeur.

There are fleeting pluses to be found throughout the runtime. The movie, for instance, features one moment of evocative suggestion — a harsh cut from a nightmarish vision to an empty throne, denoting the weight of Rama’s regal responsibility — but anything resembling moral dilemma is quickly swallowed by prescriptive plot developments, which leave little room for meaningful character arcs or emotional possibility. A climactic mid-air battle does, quite mercifully, manage to capture the imagination without much haphazard distraction. However, this scene doesn’t actually involve Rama at all. Its focus, instead, is a supporting character barely glimpsed up until that point, leaving Kapoor’s dreary protagonist to promise a more cogent narrative in the sequel.

The duology’s success is all but guaranteed in India and among its diaspora, given how deeply entrenched the Ramayana remains in Hindu and Indian culture (its release coincides with the major festival Diwali). However, to anyone not already attuned to the tale, it’s hard to imagine this version being a meaningful enough introduction to bring novice viewers back. It has neither the emotional hook nor the soul to compensate for its lack of visual momentum, and its reliance on a half-baked tech to appeal to a wider audience impedes what little drama exists at its core.
