CoD: Modern Warfare 4 Has A Thrilling Campaign, Exciting DMZ, And Strong Multiplayer

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In 2019, Infinity Ward rebooted Call of Duty's Modern Warfare series, delivering a gritty and polished campaign featuring Captain Price and the assembly of the iconic Task Force 141. Despite the strong start, the series' sequels stumbled a bit, with  Modern Warfare 3 in particular standing out as having a weak story and open-world missions that missed the mark. But with Modern Warfare 4, Infinity Ward returns the series' campaign to strength with a satisfying narrative and well-crafted missions for an epic conclusion to the rebooted series.

Modern Warfare 4's single-player goes back to being a traditional and mostly linear experience that some recent entries have strayed from. There is only one open-world mission this year, and it is far more engaging than the previous attempts. Modern Warfare 4 doesn't introduce anything new or innovative, but it finds success with a consistently strong narrative and engaging gameplay built on nail-biting tension. Thankfully, you're never forced into any mission objectives that feel like silly chores or ill-timed distractions. Every mission in Modern Warfare 4 carries urgency and drives the plot forward.

That plot closes out the ongoing narrative surrounding Price and Task Force 141, but it also takes Call of Duty to Korea and introduces a fresh new cast of characters. The campaign intertwines two seemingly separate stories for an over-the-top, globetrotting affair across New York City, Paris, and Mumbai, in addition to several parts of Korea. This is the explosive Hollywood-style action you'd expect from a Call of Duty campaign at a quality level we haven't seen from the franchise in years--that includes the recent Black Ops entries.

One half of the game places you in the boots of Private Park, a young South Korean soldier, who has never experienced real combat before. Private Park and his squad are forced to fight back to save their country when North Korea launches an invasion.

The other half of the campaign follows the events of Modern Warfare 3 and the aftermath of John "Soap" MacTavish's death. In Modern Warfare 4, Captain Price is in a much darker place, going rogue to hunt down Makarov and also tie up any other loose ends with the villain's associates.

This new outlaw version of Price ties back to one of the most-telling quotes of Modern Warfare 2019, where he lays out his ethos on where the lines in war are."You draw the line wherever you need it, Sergeant," Price says. "End of the day, someone has to make the enemy scared of the dark. We get dirty and the world stays clean. That's the mission."

That line is seemingly in a place no one could have foreseen, as Price is furious and more willing than ever to get his hands dirty in pursuit of making everyone allied with Makarov scared of the dark. Being on the run leaves Price with very few allies, though. In a desperate attempt to reach the Archer (Mads Mikkelsen), who is another threat linked to Makarov, Price makes a deal with Valeria Garza and the cartel.

Price hasn't turned into a complete monster, but the cartel don't mind blasting through civilians in order to achieve their mission. This team-up makes for chaotic gunfights through Paris and engaging banter between the characters. Price is constantly trying to look out for civilians and wants to minimize the collateral damage, but Valeria just comically taunts him, calling him Papi and old man, as she operates with less strict rules. The extent to which Price is a contrast to Valeria becomes a key part of the story. After a chase scene where they commit a particularly brutal act of violence, Valeria tells Price that "he's still a good guy," but she is a notorious cartel leader trying to get paid for delivering a weapon to the bad guy, so how much weight can her words really carry?

Price's cartel team-up also leads him to realize this weapon delivery is dangerous enough to shift the balance of power, setting in motion a much greater conflict. To make matters worse, Price also has a target on his back as the remaining members of his Task Force 141 team, Simon "Ghost" Riley and Kyle "Gaz" Garrick, are sent to capture or kill him.

This manhunt for Price leads to a standout mission called Gone Dark, where your POV switches to Gaz as he hunts Price inside of a dark hotel. At the start of the series, Captain Price served as a mentor and moral compass for Gaz, so there's a lot of emotional weight to the mission as Gaz, and the player, see just how far Price has fallen from a clearer third-party perspective. There are shocking moments and intense exchanges between the now fractured members of Task Force 141 that leverage the squad's long history effectively. There's an emotional pull throughout the mission, as Price begs Ghost and Gaz to back off, and a bigger gut punch when Price says he doesn't want to kill them, but implies he will. These moments really nail down how Price's moral compass has broken. The events of Gone Dark also set up a powerful moment in a later mission to bring the series full circle in a way I appreciated.

The Korean invasion intertwines with Price's epic revenge tour, causing the new South Korean squad and US forces to team up. Although that may seem overwhelming, even with these shifting POVs and a non-chronological story, the narrative flows well from mission to mission, keeping things moving at a good clip through stealth missions, on-foot and vehicular chase sequences, slow-motion breach-and-clear segments, and even some satisfying action of manning a chopper gunner. There's also a Clean House-style mission with night-vision goggles, where you need to clear rooms with extreme trigger discipline to avoid shooting any civilians.

Although the missions are a mostly familiar affair, they remain effective Call of Duty staples and the  campaign as a whole had me on the edge of my seat throughout. It doesn't necessarily do anything better than some of Call of Duty's most notable other campaigns, but it's proof that, for me, the series doesn't need to reinvent the wheel to produce a thrilling single-player experience. In this instance, having a strong narrative and compelling characters carried me through the missions. Whether I was playing as Park or Price, I always felt the weight of my actions.

The game's only open-world mission is called Stranded, and this is definitely one of Call of Duty's best attempts at a fully open-world mission. You play as Park, who is stranded behind enemy lines with his squad in a snowy and mountainous setting in Korea. Park needs to scavenge supplies to bring back to his squad, and there are three main objectives on the map. You can choose to complete these objectives in any order, but more notably, you can fast travel directly to each one and bypass the entire scavenging and exploration part of the mission. Doing this means you won't have the best guns and gear to complete your objectives, and it will mostly feel like you're navigating through a standard mission instead of going through a vast open-world experience you may not be interested in.

If you choose to explore in Stranded, there are bags of supplies to scavenge all around the map, and there are sealed shipping containers with combination lock puzzles you can solve to secure even more loot. The supplies you bring back to your squad can be used to repair suppressed weapons, craft lethal equipment, or utilize a mortar strike to help get the objectives done with more firepower or stealth, depending on your preferred playstyle. While I'm relieved the entire campaign isn't built on open exploration, there was an allure to the optional scavenging for weapon upgrades in Stranded. By undertaking a little of it, I looted enough supplies to secure Park a suppressed sniper rifle that proved its worth later. But for the sake of time, I fast traveled to my objectives with little consequence, calling into question the value of even having the option. At the very least, the open section introduces a replayability factor, for those that want it.

Modern Warfare 4 emphasizes boss-fight-style encounters where you only have your fists or a knife equipped and go toe-to-toe with an opponent. In these scenarios, of which there are a few, timing your blocks is critical, allowing you to parry and counter with hits of your own. Admittedly, these aren't overly complex fights, but they serve as a good way to mix things up and each one adds just the right amount of tension to the scenario and the campaign overall, especially when they tie into the competing ideologies that character dynamics are built on.

Modern Warfare 4

Modern Warfare 4 also keeps the optional dialogue prompts introduced in Modern Warfare 2, which allow you to ask your fellow soldiers questions or make small talk. You can choose to say nothing, but the banter usually adds a small layer of depth to the supporting characters and sometimes lightens the mood after an intense mission. It's a small amount of agency in what is essentially a rollercoaster that is appreciated, even if it doesn't really amount to anything deeper.

One mission places you in the boots of a high-ranked North Korean soldier who doesn't always agree with the orders he's given. This part of the story is a harrowing mission filled with public executions, but it does make use of the dialogue prompts as you walk around interrogating civilians, providing the opportunity to define the demeanour of the character a little more. The conversation prompts let you choose how to approach many situations. You can intimidate or try to calm people with the dialogue prompts you're given, but you can also just pull out your gun and threaten them with action instead of words. These small interactions don't impact the overall story and oftentimes your actions are fruitless, but it's good to have the option to choose empathy when dealing with civilians. Ultimately, this mission also does a good job of showing the ruthless nature of this North Korean threat, and how many soldiers will still blindly follow their orders.

While I'm more emotionally invested in the Task Force 141 storyline, I still enjoyed all of the missions focused on the South Korean squad. Park is forced into the military through conscription, so being a soldier wasn't a choice or an act of valor. Initially, it seems like he doesn't want to be a soldier at all, but when war hits his doorstep, he still tries to push back his fear and make the best decisions he can to protect his people. Park also provides a nice moral balance to Price's side of the campaign, where nothing matters except his thirst for revenge.

Despite the campaign otherwise being a blast, the final "boss fights" of the game are a major disappointment. Neither fight feels particularly challenging, and they feel too brief for the climactic moments, but the way the story concludes is satisfying all the same.

Overall, Modern Warfare 4 wraps up the series with several shocking moments and ends on a high note. The pacing never falters despite the two interwoven storylines and alternating POVs, and the wide variety of missions and locations ensure that the story remains engaging. Modern Warfare 4 is another edge-of-the-seat thrill ride. What distinguishes its campaign from others is a feeling that the devs had more time to think through the experience to create something that can serve as a worthy conclusion for the Modern Warfare series. Because of that, Modern Warfare 4's campaign ranks as the best we've seen since Modern Warfare 2019.

Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 - DMZ Mode Review

Building on the success of the DMZ beta launched in 2022, Infinity Ward elevates the extraction mode with major upgrades for Modern Warfare 4, including a more dynamic map, an engaging social hub, in-depth story missions, and deeper progression. All of this allows for more replayability and new ways to experience the sandbox magic that can happen within a match of DMZ. In fact, most of Infinity Ward's Call of Duty innovation this year is packed within DMZ, and thanks to a satisfying gameplay loop, the extraction mode is the standout part of Modern Warfare 4.

Matches of DMZ allow for up to 60 players to group up in trios. Each squad is tasked with surviving and extracting before a circle of irradiated gas floods the map, killing everyone in its path. This is PvPvE gameplay with a focus on exploration, combat, and a wide variety of missions. As with most extraction shooters, dying in DMZ means losing everything, making it a high-risk for high-reward game mode.

DMZ's new Hajin map is a vast upgrade from the original Al Mazrah location. This is the largest map ever created for Call of Duty, outsizing Warzone's Verdansk and even Black Ops 7's massive Avalon map, allowing for deeper exploration and greater map diversity. Hajin is set in a fictional version of the demilitarized zone between North and South Korea, and the area provides a wide range of main and secondary points-of-interest.

In the hours I spent playing DMZ, my squadmates and I always seemed to spawn in a new area. No match ever felt the same, and there was always so much more we wanted to explore before our time ran out. Al Mazrah's diversity of locations doesn't even come close to Hajin, which takes you through dense forests, as well as a selection of city-based locations, such as military compounds, underground tunnel systems, and even an irradiated nuclear power plant.

Updated NPC behavior and dynamic weather make Hajin feel more alive than Al Mazrah. Rain or fog can roll in at any time but aren't guaranteed in every match. Weather changes don't affect the entire map and are randomly localized to specific areas, shifting how you experience Hajin from point-to-point. Fog can be beneficial for stealth as it hinders the view of everyone in the vicinity, including NPC enemies, but it also limits your ability to check your surroundings. Rain on the ground (or traversing through other water sources) can create audible sloshy footsteps, betraying your position. I'm always skeptical about dynamic weather in games, but Modern Warfare 4's graphics are stunning, and it was impressive watching the fog roll across the coast and move inland. And on at least one occasion, driving through dense fog allowed my squad to evade an entire convoy of enemies. Fog is more impactful than the rain, but both are a welcome touch to the world, as they can change how you approach a situation, and they add a bit of immersion and variety to matches.

With Hajin, you'll see enemies moving through the world in a more organic way. At military bases, you might find soldiers doing push-ups outside in the yard, while patrol cars full of enemies drive around the map. Drones search the sky in various locations, suicide-bomber robo-dogs can chase you around, and a boss-level tank patrols the streets. The AI threats aren't smarter, but these behaviors and a wide range of enemy types provide a sense of immersion and make for engaging combat encounters. You also can't underestimate them entirely because of their lack of intelligence in combat, as they can easily overwhelm you with their numbers. In the higher-tier zones, the NPCs have heavier armor and some wield riot shields, so it takes firepower and strategy to take them down.

Changes to progression make the experience much more rewarding. You can load into a match with the intent of looting cash and resources, hunting other players, engaging in story missions, or completing smaller side objectives. No matter how you choose to play, all of these actions count toward your DMZ player level, and you earn this XP even if you fail to successfully extract. You'll also earn player XP and weapon XP for multiplayer and Warzone, but you can only rank up your DMZ level by playing DMZ.

Modern Warfare 4's DMZ has a bigger focus on mission prepping, and this is handled through the new FOB (Forward Operating Base) feature. The FOB is your personal home base between missions. This is a place you can walk around and interact with various mission-prep stations, meaning no one has to sit in boring menus or lobby screens while their teammates get their loadouts ready. You don't need to load in and out of the firing range feature like you do in multiplayer, as there's a shooting range inside the FOB, so you can walk up and use it for target practice. Teammates are added to the FOB of whoever is hosting the party, so there is a social element as well, but this base is also a crucial command center that evolves with you as you level up in DMZ. This adds another layer to DMZ's engaging gameplay loop, as you're constantly trying to level up and unlock helpful stations and upgrades within your FOB, including a vendor, bounty board, dog tag case, and more importantly, a 3D printer for crafting crucial gear.

There are no Buy Stations on the map this year, meaning you can't purchase what you need during the match. This is a positive change that prevents everyone from getting their hands on powerful items right away. You'll have base weapons and gear to start, but you'll need to do some scavenging in your matches in order to get better equipped. This change puts more emphasis on looting and exploration, requiring you to search out guns and gear in-match, or at the very least, find the resources to craft what you need. Crafting is done through the FOB's 3D printer, using looted materials to print essential items like backpacks, armor, and parachutes.

DMZ also no longer lets you build custom weapons without paying to do so. Once you've reached level 10, you'll unlock the Gunsmith FOB station. This lets you buy and customize base weapons, but you'll be limited to a small selection of guns until you've scavenged weapon manuals to unlock more. The base price for guns isn't pricey, but you'll need to pay to unlock attachment slots as well as each individual attachment you want to include. Slots also get more expensive, so you can end up spending a lot to fully customize a single gun that you risk losing every time you load into a match. You can choose to load into a match with a free random loadout consisting of the most basic gun and supplies, but ultimately, the Gunsmith change adds more weight to your decision to risk something expensive or play it safe with less-effective gear.

Leveling operators in the beta was simplistic, but MW4 gives you three different trait trees you can invest in: Warfighter, Scout, and Survivalist. The longer an operator stays alive, the more powerful they become and the more upgrades they can acquire, but dying in Hajin means you've lost that specific operator inside the DMZ zone. This sounds devastating if you've invested time into leveling up an operator and upgrading their traits, but they're not permanently lost. You have the option to spend in-game money to send an evac team to rescue them from Hajin. This can get pricey if they are a high-level operator, but it's a relief to have the option to salvage something from your loss.

There are often several layers of complexity to extraction modes, so they can feel overwhelming and confusing at first. DMZ's menus and mission deployment options are no different in that regard, especially with all the new bells and whistles baked into the mode. You are onboarded with a brief tutorial mission to teach you the ropes, but this mostly teaches you how to follow mission markers and heal yourself after a fight. Beyond that, DMZ offers various mission types to choose from, and it's initially difficult to differentiate what each type offers.

There are currently only six main story missions available, but they are engaging multi-step story objectives. One mission sent us on a fun casino heist, where we had to locate and breach a vault, hijacking as many bags of cash as we could before irradiated gas spread through the vault. Another tasked my squad with locating a hostage in a hospital, who was handcuffed to a gurney, and we had to wheel them out to an exfil location while enemy forces made our exit difficult. I wish there were more than six missions, but they were a blast to play, and you can opt for different strategies to complete them. 

The charm of DMZ remains the random and chaotic moments you get with the sandbox nature of the mode, and the living world of Hajin definitely elevates the unpredictability, allowing every playthrough to feel unique. The world is also filled with smaller missions and objectives, so there are still many ways to play and level up.

DMZ addresses the elephant in the room, which is the growing divide between players in extraction shooters. Some players believe the games should always have a PvP focus while others want the extraction experience in a PvE environment. DMZ attempts to appeal to both sides with its approach to PvP. Killing players now activates a heat level, which is similar to the Wanted system in GTA, and you'll earn a marked bounty on your head. The more players you kill, the higher your Wanted bounty grows. Hajin has bounty board stations, where it shows all active players in the match who are marked as Wanted. This bounty board lets you either pay cash to reduce your own heat level, or you can buy intel to locate the other Wanted players. This feels like a great way to cater to the PvP-focused extraction players, allowing them to hunt each other down for large sums of money, while passive players can stay off the radar if they avoid killing other operators.

Another major change this year is that DMZ is no longer a free-to-play mode tied to Warzone, and instead is packed into Modern Warfare 4 as part of its $70 price tag. While losing a free game is always a bummer, the amount of upgrades packed into DMZ do feel worthy of a premium buy-in.

While it's not pay-to-win per se, DMZ does provide additional benefits to players who purchase the pricey Vault edition of Modern Warfare 4. This gives them access to an additional operator slot, two medium-size backpacks, two armor-plate carriers, and other beneficial but single-useitems. Vault edition owners getting these freebies isn't a major concern, as the specific items don't have a huge impact on whether or not you lose a fight against them. However, this gets tackier when the game stops being free-to-play, and there's always the worry it's a downhill slide toward further bonuses that do become pay-to-win. Hopefully this doesn't lead to players getting bigger advantages such as UAVs or high-rarity guns. In-game bonuses are best handed out as cosmetic items like operator and vehicle skins.

Once you get past the confusing mission lingo, DMZ settles into a sandbox experience with all the thrills of a high-risk/high-reward extraction shooter. Despite the original DMZ being an engaging concept, it struggled with barebones features and weak missions, but you can see how it served as the building blocks for Modern Warfare 4's more robust and engaging gameplay loop. Much of the magic often comes from the chaos that builds as you fight or flee through dangerous areas of Hajin, and the new progression layers make it easy to get lost in the world of DMZ. Overall, Infinity Ward has elevated Call of Duty's extraction mode with engaging features and riveting gameplay, and I can't wait to jump into my next match on Hajin.

Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 - Multiplayer Review

After two years of Black Ops games, the removal of omnimovement creates an adjustment period for Modern Warfare 4's multiplayer. That system made it easy to sprint, jump, dive, and slide in any direction, but Modern Warfare 4's movement offers fewer options. Several times, I caught myself trying to perform a wall jump or attempt a backward dive to avoid an enemy's grenade. But once I overcame this omnimovement muscle memory, Modern Warfare 4's multiplayer felt a lot like Modern Warfare 3.

Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 ditches the advanced omnimovement system without compromising the overall feel of high-speed movement. While multiplayer offers the familiar fast-paced action and strong gunplay you'd expect from a Call of Duty game, a more significant innovation lies in the fresh and ever-changing Kill Block map.

The less agile, more grounded approach to movement sounds like it could be a step backward, but it isn't necessarily a bad thing. Modern Warfare 4's movement is still fast and responsive. Infinity Ward has implemented smoother mantling and sliding mechanics this year, including the ability to seamlessly transition from mantling over objects into a slide, and there's also a supine slide that lets you get under low-hanging obstacles. These new movement tweaks allow you to chain various actions together in a satisfyingly fluid way.

So movement is still pretty speedy and fluid, but players who want to opt for a more methodical approach can still play slowly and hold their ground without being too overwhelmed by fast-moving and agile sweaty players--ditching omnimovement has removed the irritation of facing any "omnimovement kings," who hop, flop, and wall jump all over the map. Overall, this movement system feels like a great middle ground between the players who want the fast and twitchy approach of the new era and the old school players who prefer the more grounded feel.

There is also the new ability to climb up pipes, which sounds silly, but this is Modern Warfare 4's way of making elevated areas just as accessible as they were with the wall jump in Black Ops 7, without undermining the grounded contemporary setting. You can't wall-tap into a window anymore, but you can climb a pipe to sneak up and reach an enemy on an upper level. Climbing pipes is a risk that leaves you vulnerable, but it's a quick shimmy up, so you're not in danger for long. Pipe climbing is a smart way to provide verticality in a Modern Warfare setting, which players have come to expect now.

As for the maps, there's a solid variety of designs, ranging from small to medium-sized standard maps set under the bright lights of New York City, in a snowy region of Korea, and in a colorfully decorated village in Mumbai. There are also a few large-scale Ground War maps played on chunks of the DMZ mode's Hajin map, for bigger infantry battles and the addition of vehicular warfare. The diversity in battlefields is enough to cater to various playstyles and shouldn't get boring quickly. However, the real standout is Kill Block,  Infinity Ward's ambitious Gunfight map.

Kill Block's size is equivalent to the popular Shoot House map from 2019's Modern Warfare, but the design consists of three modular pieces that can be swapped out and connected with other sections in different ways. Between each match and during rounds of Gunfight, in which two teams try to eliminate each other and players only get one life per round, the active three pieces decouple and a new configuration is set. When Infinity Ward first announced Kill Block, I was skeptical of how the studio would execute this living map with a vast amount of potential configurations, but the modular swaps between rounds feel seamless, and all of these movable parts fit surprisingly well together.

Kill Block is a blast to play and it's easily one of the best innovations of this rebooted Modern Warfare series. Every new configuration keeps you on your toes, as you can't anticipate which three pieces will get stitched together. The map chunks also include iconic designs, so longtime Call of Duty fans get the treat of enjoying classic maps in new ways. For example, a piece of Shoot House might be combined with a section of Storage Town from Warzone's Verdansk and Highrise from the original Modern Warfare 2. Kill Block features matches of 10v10 and 6v6 Gunfight, but I hope this map is eventually integrated into standard game modes, too.

The Gunsmith weapon customization feature returns in MW4 and it adds the new virtual Gunny assistant to help you build loadouts. This isn't some wonky AI feature that the name evokes, but  a set of prebuilt loadouts the developer has baked into the game to help suggest weapon builds based on playstyle. For example, if you like the M4 assault rifle, Gunny can suggest builds depending on whether you want the gun customized for short-range, to be balanced, or for long-range combat. This new Gunsmith assistant didn't always suggest attachments I liked or thought were the best overall builds, but I think Gunny will be great for casual players looking to jump into matches faster without needing to worry too much about crafting the perfect "meta" weapon build.

Another positive change for weapon customization is the addition of Apex attachments. Every primary and secondary weapon (excluding launchers) has one unique add-on designed to drastically change how the weapon is used, similar to the weapon conversion kits found in the recent Call of Duty games. But in Modern Warfare 4, they don't cost an attachment slot, so you can have a five-attachment build and still convert your gun with its Apex attachment. This creates more of a reason to try something unconventional than there has been in past titles.

The Oden's Apex attachment is one of my favorites, as it transforms the automatic assault rifle into a powerful single-fire sniper. This isn't ideal for every mode, but instead something I equip for more power and precision when playing on Modern Warfare 4's large-scale Ground War maps. 

While Oden has one of the more practical Apex attachments, some are a bit wacky and will cater to very niche playstyles and interests. The Oris 8.6 marksman rifle's Razor attachment, for example, gives you the option to shoot throwing knives in close-range combat. Again, it's less than practical in many situations, but these are fun attachments that won't cost you anything to try.

Weapon leveling is a component of Call of Duty that has been a huge slog in recent years. Guns always have dozens of attachments, and it then takes hours of usage to make any real progress leveling them up and accessing the attachments you want. One major improvement in Modern Warfare 4 is the reduced weapon-leveling grind. Guns have several universal attachments now and unlocking these for one gun means you don't have to suffer the grind for that same attachment with other weapons. This shortens the list of attachments and levels you need to progress through, making it easier to tailor your ideal gun. 

Call of Duty multiplayer always features either a killstreak or scorestreak system to calculate how you earn the game's lethal or support streaks. Modern Warfare 4 is more of a killstreak-based system, but it does award some points for objective-based actions such as grabbing tags in Kill Confirmed or capturing a Domination point. However, this doesn't feel rewarding as a hybrid system, as the points feel miniscule for that team player who is capturing and defending objectives. Earning streaks in Modern Warfare 4 is too heavily reliant on your ability to chain together kills. I'm always someone who prefers scorestreaks, as it better  incentivizes you and your teammates to fight for the objectives and play for the win. Modern Warfare 2 best settled the debate of killstreaks versus scorestreaks by offering the option to choose the system you preferred, allowing you to toggle on killstreaks or scoresteaks at any time. Modern Warfare 4 unsuccessfully attempts to bridge the gap with the hybrid system, and I hope a toggle can be added to give player choice.

Outside of playing Kill Block, much of multiplayer's excitement comes from Modern Warfare 4's new objective game modes. Call of Duty's staples are still available with Team Deathmatch, Domination, Hardpoint, Kill Confirmed, and Search and Destroy, but Infinity Ward cooked up new modes that breathe more heart-pounding action into multiplayer with Inflation and Collateral.

Inflation is a more intense, cash-based spin on Kill Confirmed. Instead of collecting dog tags from fallen players, you're picking up cash. Any player can collect fallen cash from someone's death, and the team holding the most money at the end of the match wins. I really love the momentum swings you get during the late game stage of Inflation, as players who accumulate large sums of money can cause a significant shift if they die and drop a portion of their cash. With less than a minute left of your game, you can go from drastically losing to winning in an instant, and that's excitement you don't get in a match of Kill Confirmed.

Collateral is a round-based mode which puts a twist on Call of Duty's iconic Search and Destroy, adding a cash system similar to Counter-Strike. Much like Search and Destroy, teams alternate between attacking and defending two bomb sites, but killstreaks, automatic health regeneration, and custom loadouts are all disabled. Instead, everyone starts with a pistol and earns in-game cash throughout the match to spend on strategic upgrades between rounds. You lose your loadout if you die, but all earned money is kept between rounds. Castlevania: Belmont’s Curse Is The Series At Its Best

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When playing Castlevania: Belmont's Curse, I couldn't stop thinking about Symphony of the Night. Almost 30 years ago, that game reinvented Castlevania as a search-action game, and did it so brilliantly that it earned half of the "metroidvania" portmanteau. Belmont's Curse does not attempt such a dramatic shift. It doesn't change genres or reinvent the series as something entirely new. What it does, however, is achieve a brilliant synthesis, with every component operating at peak performance to deliver a new high for the series. Castlevania is back, and it is fantastic.

Put simply, Belmont's Curse is the total package. A sprawling map bursting with mysteries to uncover, joyous traversal tools that make roaming the map feel great, a story that balances a macabre tone with occasional lightness, a cast of creative and well-drawn characters, and hands-down the best combat of any Castlevania game--in fact, among the best combat of any 2D action game, period.

What makes all of these synergize so well is the addition of the tarot cards, which serve as an elegant way to fuse the game's disparate elements, and makes them all feel like part of one cohesive whole. At different points your tarot deck serves as mission signposting, environmental puzzles, pieces of story lore, characters to serve as dialogue for Rose, boss locations, and ultimately, your pool of spells.

You play as Rose Belmont, daughter of Trevor Belmont, who has trained her whole life as the successor of the Belmont clan. Though silver-maned Trevor is still a capable fighter, Rose volunteers to investigate the otherworldly happenings in Paris, France, eager to prove herself worthy of the family name. But Rose is also afflicted with a cursed red mark on one arm, making her dangerously instrumental to the machinations of Dracula and his armies. 

The tarot deck is a set of magical cards gifted to Rose by her late mother, Sypha, who passed on the curse to her at birth. Each card represents a follower of Dracula, with honorific names like The Fallen, The Warrior, or The Builder. As you explore the castle you find clues that reveal story details and hints about how to find them, often both simultaneously, and they each have their own roles in Dracula's royal court. When Dracula's castle emerges in the heart of Paris--as you obviously knew it would, this is a Castlevania game, after all--the full deck is revealed, putting the complete array of monsters in your sights. Once you've tracked down and defeated one of Dracula's servants, their tarot card becomes an Arcana you can use as a spell, which then opens its own flexible progression system. It may sound complex, but the system is actually an ingenious way to merge different gameplay elements and make them all feel digestible and cohesive.

Castlevania: Belmont's Curse

As an example, take the first boss, The Fallen. Finding him is a relatively simple task, given that it's meant to serve as a basic primer for finding clues and navigating the winding streets of Paris. When you defeat him, you absorb his Arcana, which grants you two important tools: a recognizable boomerang axe spell, and an arcane whip that allows you to grab onto grapple points to reach new locations. But this new traversal tool is also a combat tool, since the same function can grab onto any enemy; a well-timed strike while grappling toward the enemy can then do serious damage. Then on top of that, the combat tool itself can also be used for traversal, as enemies flying midair can help you reach distant platforms, as long as you can grapple onto them. It all harmonizes beautifully, and exemplifies Belmont's Curse as a whole--abundant with mechanics that complement and fold into others in surprising ways.

Dracula's legion of followers also serve another purpose, as being captured forces them into the tarot where they occupy a mindspace for Rose to speak with. This ultimately gives her characters to bounce off of for story purposes, but the characters are also well differentiated from each other. The Fallen is a gruff creature that comes to feel protective of Rose, whereas Medusa, even imprisoned in Rose's deck, is unsubtly rooting for her to fail and constantly trying to trick her into making moves that will further her master's plans. Lots of characters you find throughout have their own agendas and rivalries, and are often at cross-purposes with Rose or each other, adding a compelling dynamic as they all bicker inside of your head. The regular addition of characters in your deck means that any of them could interject in dialogue to offer some commentary--though the core cast, so to speak, remains the first three that you find. 

One drawback of having such a dynamic cast of characters is that the protagonist, Rose, is less interesting by comparison. She's a determined heroine archetype and remains that way throughout. She is a central point of gravity for all of the other characters to orbit around, and works on that level, but I do wish she had more agency herself. She is deadset on her mission from start to finish, and not much more.

Each Arcana spell also comes with a set of three objectives to complete, called Works of Mercy. These can be as simple as defeating a set number of enemies with the relevant spell, but often are more complex to incentivize exploration or experimentation. One notable objective for The Warrior rewards you for finding a particular location, which works thematically with that character's story. One of Medusa's has you petrify an enemy carrying a shield, a reference to the myth of Perseus. Your reward for completing these is opening a node to choose one of three power-ups for the spell; once you've completed all three, you're rewarded with extra dialogue from the Arcana character. 

And so you explore the streets and undercity of Paris along with Dracula's castle in freeform fashion, an open structure lightly guides you toward critical objectives for unlocking new equipment that you need to reach other corners and layers of the world. If you're ever stuck you can refer to your tarot deck for clues that you may not have thought about for a while. The environmental puzzles add just enough brain-tickling challenge to make you observe carefully and think through the clues, without grinding your progress to a halt. And the ability to track down enemies and steal their powers makes for a compelling loop that reminded me of one of my favorite series: Mega Man. I was always eager to take down the next boss and see how they would augment my combat and traversal abilities. 

Combat is where you can truly see the expertise of developer Evil Empire shine through the most, as its expertise from Dead Cells transfers beautifully to this franchise. Your strikes across the wide variety of weapon types feel forceful and kinetic, and each weapon type has its own advantages and special charged ability. Your basic longsword is well balanced and lets you dash through enemies, while the whip has long reach but only the tip strikes with its full power. I found I favored the lance, with its versatility of long reach as a handheld weapon or the ability to throw it as a projectile, and ultimately stuck with it for a long time even after finding other weapons at higher levels. There are four weapons each across seven type categories, so the seven weapon types offer loads of room for experimentation to dial in on the one you like best, or just swapping situationally.

What makes the combat transcendant, though, is the addition of a parry. This is new ground for a Castlevania game, but it also works differently than the parry I've seen in many other games. As a holy shield gained from your second boss encounter, it works independently of your regular strikes. It doesn't interrupt your attacks, and by default the attack button is mapped to a face button while the parry is mapped to the left trigger. That means you can be attacking while also parrying your opponent's attack, making combat a lightning fast series of choices that puts you on offense and defense simultaneously. As someone who has struggled with many parry systems and never quite got into them in other games, this one made a believer out of me. It just feels so good to wail on an enemy, parry a projectile from behind, and then grapple over to finish them off. Belmont's Curse is full of moments like this, as practically every room you enter is occupied by enemies, and frequent forced combat encounters test your mettle against mixtures of them.

This is not to say combat is easy. Far from it. The cursed streets of Paris and Dracula's castle are littered with monsters and ghouls familiar to Castlevania fans, reimagined for this combat style, and Rose is very vulnerable. It's easy to die even in regular combat encounters, and boss fights take a good bit of pattern recognition to learn tells before you can successfully fend them off. But the combat is so fine-tuned that you constantly feel in control and recognize what you did wrong. Very occasionally the screen would be so full of enemy attacks I missed a parry window because it was hard to read all the visual information at once, but it was rare enough not to detract from the overall experience. Similarly, navigating extended gauntlets of traps can be trying, but they always feel satisfying to complete, and you're rewarded with items like ability-augmenting Relics or further clues for your tarot deck.

If combat or traps give you trouble, Belmont's Curse adds a few quality-of-life features and options to ease your growth. For one, you keep all the XP earned during your runbacks to bosses, so even if you have to try a boss multiple times, you're still leveling up and gaining power on the way. You can also turn on the Custom Mode to tweak options like setting a checkpoint right in front of boss doors, boosting your damage output, reducing the damage from traps, and more. There doesn't seem to be any cost to using this system, and you can turn it on or off as you'd like. The one element missing, for good reason, are any extra navigational tools to find your way. It's clear that Evil Empire doesn't mind giving you a leg up on reflex-based challenges, but it still wants you to think through puzzles and explore to uncover secrets.

Belmont's Curse also has some of the most inventive bosses I've seen in the series history. One notable boss twice escaped to a different section, introducing a difficult platforming challenge in the middle of the fight to continue it. Another hexes you with a sigil indicating the number of hits you can take before you simply die, forcing you to minimize strikes on you even if you have plenty of healing items to prevent dying outright. Even after 20 hours there are many other bosses in my deck I have yet to find, and I'm eager to see what else Castlevania has to offer.

Exploring the castle is a visual delight, because Belmont's Curse is gorgeous. Far from a gray-soaked castle, Evil Empire has leaned into its house art style of vivid, saturated hues and unexpected color palettes. The approach to Dracula's castle, in which Rose and all of the enemies are silhouetted against the pink-red color tone of a burning Paris behind them, is one of the most visually arresting moments I've seen in a game lately. The character designs themselves are distinct and creative, like Carmilla, a hemomancer whose slinky red gown is made of dripping blood. I could go on and on, but I'll let you discover them for yourself. Plus as you explore you get treated to new renditions of classic Castlevania music alongside new tunes that fit right into its milieu. 

Every element in Castlevania: Belmont's Curse works so well both independently and as part of the whole: flexible combat, beautifully acrobatic traversal, fine-tuned environmental puzzles, wide cast of interesting characters, and eye-catching visual panache--all wrapped within a smart tarot system that lets these ideas complement each other. Belmont's Curse feels destined to be regarded alongside Symphony of the Night as one of the best Castlevania games, and one of the best metroidvania games, of all time.

Valor Mortis Is A First-Person Soulslike That Tries Too Much

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The soulslike genre has been around long enough now that it's easy to pick out its most recognizable traits. Whether it's managing stamina in combat, the risk of losing progression with each death, health items and enemies that respawn when you reach a bonfire, lantern, or some other recognizable checkpoint; all of the best in the genre adapt these in their own distinct ways. 

Valor Mortis is no different, but attempts to stand out through its shift in perspective. By moving the action to a first-person view, Valor Mortis affords itself novelty, which works well with snappy and satisfying combat that rewards deft parries and patience in equal measure. But it's also marred by a weak story and sluggishly paced opening hours that betray the more captivating moments in its second half, where Valor Mortis' most interesting narrative beats and combat encounters finally deliver on some of the potential you can see being established in the hours prior.

You play as William Dowsett, a very British-sounding member of Napoleon's army who returns from the dead only to find former allies taken over by corruption sweeping across Europe. The corruption has twisted people; large red boils replace the heads of riflemen, infantrymen have their spines snapped as they are transformed into grotesque and rabid attack dogs, and piles of bodies have become living masses of flesh that slowly crawl toward you when you least expect it. It's a familiar world that has been transformed into a nightmare for William as he attempts to reconnect with missing comrades and understand how the war has found itself in this state, leading him to answers that will likely be far less surprising for you as a player.

Valor Mortis' story is frustratingly straightforward, and wastes a setting so rarely used in action games. William's slow realization that the war he's been waging on behalf of the bloodthirsty Napoleon Bonaparte might not be as noble as he assumed is annoyingly drawn out for how predictable it is. It's hard to get invested with a main character who has little more going for him than verbalizing his disgust over the consequences of a war he so recently was proud to be a part of, blissfully ignorant to the horrors he was unleashing on innocent citizens along the way. Conversations with the game's supporting cast don't help either, with none making a lasting impression. 

Once Valor Mortis starts exploring the villages and cities besieged by Bonaparte and his corrupted forces, there are more compelling stories to discover. You'll come across brief echoes that capture fleeting conversations from the past, distilling short but often heartbreaking stories. They effectively convey the desperation some civilians endured when faced with a foreign, oppressive force taking over their day-to-day lives. There's an entire city filled with orphans who scurry around underground tunnels, and another nearly entirely underwater after a heinous war crime extinguished a rebellion without care for civilian casualties. They're effective at contextualizing why so much of the world around you is desolate and broken without having to see those events play out, and they do a great job of making you hate an antagonist you'll hardly ever see.

It's frustrating that it takes a while to get here, however, with many of Valor Mortis' opening hours taking place across battlefields that, while twisted by the corruption around them, ultimately blend together in forgettable ways. These areas are almost strictly linear, with clear paths from beginning to end and only slight detours to explore. Around the halfway mark, this monotonous world design flips around entirely--you move from abandoned villages to burning cities and down into labyrinthine catacombs beneath an eerily empty university. These spaces feel more like small hubs, with smart shortcuts back to resting points (represented here as lanterns, which restock your healing vials and respawn enemies as you might expect) that make them feel more carefully considered in comparison to the opening hours.

Valor Mortis' combat is where it takes its biggest swings. Its shift to a first-person perspective is the most immediate and distinct change from typical soulslikes, but its split between melee and ranged combat and multiple weapon options are equally impactful. It is still all about crowd and stamina control, as well as a familiar reliance on parries, too. The tighter field of view makes it important to intelligently choose which enemies to engage and when, though off-screen markers  help alleviate potential frustrations the perspective change would otherwise introduce. Melee combat is fast and responsive, with parries being especially satisfying to pull off given the tight windows that make many duels, especially those against bosses, exhilarating. I rarely tired of hearing the sound cues of a critical attack, be it from a stealth attack or through breaking their posture, accompanied by violent, contextual executions that varied based on the weapons I had equipped. Ranged combat is given a greater emphasis than most other soulslike games too, and you'll routinely be challenged to pick off enemies from afar while juggling those within striking distance. 

"By moving the action to a first-person view, Valor Mortis affords itself novelty, which works well with snappy and satisfying combat that rewards deft parries and patience in equal measure"

There is a range of offensive weapons and abilities on offer, each of which is introduced as you progress through the story. Included are three types of swords: a rapier that excels at parries and building posture damage, a two-handed claymore that forgoes parries entirely in favor of slow, powerful, and magically imbued attacks, and a well-rounded saber that balances damage and posture damage. Firearms are similarly varied, with a quick-firing pistol, a slow but powerful blunderbuss that's devastating at close range, and a two-handed musket that's great for picking off enemies from afar. You're also granted three transmutations, or magical abilities, which allow you to hurl fire at enemies, freeze them with stasis, or absorb and reflect their damage back at them, as well as the ability to wall-run (which feels just as good as it did in developer One More Level's Ghostrunner series).

Each of these weapons and abilities feature its own upgrade trees, with specific materials required to unlock each sequential node. I ended up finding enough materials to nearly max out each upgrade tree without specifically hunting for them, which allowed me the freedom to experiment with each one without having to commit to specific configurations. This seems by design, given that you'll always have one sword equipped in your main hand and either a firearm or spell in your off hand, similar to Dishonored or BioShock 2, as well as the ability to swap either one at any time.

Despite the freedom of choice, I was surprised by how little I swapped between the various options, especially when it came to both swords and magical abilities. The saber, which is the first weapon you're given, fulfilled my needs so perfectly that I only briefly dabbled with both the rapier and claymore before changing back. The rapier's slightly additional posture damage never made up for its inability to block attacks without perfect parries, and the claymore is introduced so late and is incapable of performing parries entirely, stripping out one of the most satisfying elements of the entire combat loop. 

The same goes for the three transmutations, as I found myself mostly sticking to fire given its effectiveness against most enemies. Building up enough stasis on enemies consumed more nephtoglobin, or mana, than it seemed worthwhile, and the act of absorbing and returning damage with the third ability always felt more clunky in practice and less effective than just sticking to the far more satisfying parrying mechanic. These two other abilities are utilized more often in environmental puzzles, though these are predominantly used to initially explain how the abilities work. They go mostly ignored afterward, annoyingly not building on the gameplay design introduced early on.

Enemy variety is partly to blame. Valor Mortis features a relatively shallow pool of foes and takes a long time to start combining them in ways that make you consider how to approach a fight in specific ways. You'll be fighting the same slow-moving and weak infantryman in your first and last hours of the game, which saps some of the satisfaction out of the combat when you're pulling off the same parries against the same attacks for hours on end. In a similar fashion to the level design, this starts resolving itself when you have the full complement of weapons and abilities at your disposal, at which point enemy combinations and slight variations start becoming more abundant. With the added benefit of more open spaces to explore and more opportunities to deliver surprises with enemy placements, Valor Mortis' combat eventually starts to shine. It's just a pity that it takes some time to get there.

That might be a blessing or a curse depending on what you're coming to Valor Mortis for, but it has a noticeable knock-on effect that's difficult to ignore. Runbacks after death were far less tense because of how familiar and, in many cases, sparse enemies were. Character progression, which is governed by an upgrade tree with no branching paths to commit to, didn't drastically change the way I played, instead just giving me a means to buff my attributes to keep up with the escalating damage and health pools of enemies. I was rarely seeking out a specific upgrade to help deal with new foes because I had become accustomed to fighting the same ones over and over again. Thankfully boss fights offered some reprieve to this, as they tend to feature a good variety of enemies, large and lumbering to small and nimble, that inject the combat with a sense of challenge and tension. 

There are many other elements outside of combat, too, that only start to really click closer to the game's climax. Early stages are so preoccupied with introducing you to new weapons and abilities and providing you with spaces to learn each one of them that they feel disjointed and unfocused. At one point you're taking out distant groups of enemies conveniently placed next to explosive barrels to showcase the effectiveness of your new musket, and at another you're engaging in a long platforming sequence where you need to string together wall runs and grapples while maintaining pace with a moving cart through a mine shaft. Ultimately, all these issues stem from a lack of restraint; There are too many disparate ideas that feel at odds with the best aspect of Valor Mortis, making it feel directionless at times.

Valor Mortis betrays the heights it reaches in its second half. Its first-person combat is snappy and satisfying, but the limited enemy pool, underutilized weapons and abilities, and lack of consistent challenge ultimately prevent it from shining. And while this historical period is novel to explore with a dark, supernatural twist, it doesn't play host to a wider, captivating story, and is only salvaged by smaller, emotionally charged vignettes that effectively convey the consequences of a war that its protagonist so naively comes to terms with. There are the bones of a great game here--one that would benefit from a tighter focus on its engaging first-person combat and a trimming of the superfluous mechanics that detract from the ones that work. Instead, it's an uneven experience that delights as much as it disappoints.

Dragon’s Dogma 2: Dark Arisen Is Great, But At a Cost

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Capcom choosing the exact same subtitle for two separate expansions in two different games makes a lot of sense. Dragon's Dogma 2: Dark Arisen is not only a thematically relevant title, but thanks to the first game's expansion of the same name, it also conveys to longtime fans what to expect: namely, a new gameplay cycle focused on reworked loot and plenty of dungeon crawling. The key difference this time around is that instead of heading into the labyrinthine depths of a cursed island, you've got an entirely new region to explore.  

As someone who loved Dragon's Dogma 2, I couldn't really ask for much more. Yet changes to some of the game's core systems also dilute what made it so unique when it launched back in 2024. I know it's always been a fairly divisive game, and these changes are intended to make the experience more approachable, but they also strip away some of its magic in an effort to appeal to more people. Dark Arisen is still a great expansion, transporting you to a fascinating new region with more loot to earn, fantastical beasts to slay, and stories to behold, but it also shifts Dragon's Dogma 2 in a slightly different direction. 

To start with, Dark Arisen alleviates some of the game's intentional friction by reducing how often you need to travel on foot. The Eternal Ferrystone was already added to the game a few months ago, letting you freely fast travel to any portcrystal without expending a scarce resource. Those same portcrystals are located at every key location in Dark Arisen's new Norgan region. You still have to reach them on foot first to activate them, but once you do, you can simply teleport from one side of the map to the other. 

Using the Eternal Ferrystone is entirely optional; you can still traverse almost all of Norgan on foot if you desire, so the friction is still there if you want it. However, Dark Arisen also feels designed with unlimited fast travel in mind. The first settlement you come across, for instance, acts as a hub of sorts, letting you manage your gear, rest at the inn, and pick up a fair few side quests, so you're intended to return here often. 

Meanwhile, many quests require you to visit the same locations over and over again to speak with specific characters. Heading off on foot might be an option, but it's never incentivized and feels antithetical to how it wants you to play. 

I'm not a fan of this change. I can acknowledge that giving players more choice is a positive, but the back-and-forth quest design and reliance on fast travel means you never really feel like you have options when traversing Norgan. This has a fairly significant knock-on effect, too: Without embarking on foot more often, I rarely stumbled into the emergent moments that made the base game so thrilling. And without that palpable sense of adventure, Dark Arisen feels less like Dragon's Dogma and more like any number of action-RPGs, rendering this a fundamental change to its identity, rather than an improvement.

When Dragon's Dogma 2 launched, traveling the regions of Vermund and Battahl felt treacherous, especially in the black of night. The world was as hazardous as the enemies you encountered, and campsites provided a welcome respite on your weary travels. Now, those feelings of everpresent danger and welcome safety are mostly gone. There's no risk when you can freely fast travel out of danger or skip a journey entirely. The game's uncompromising design has been softened to broaden its appeal.  

The same goes for changes to its save system. By letting you roll back to a manual "Interim Save" at any time, there's no longer any risk involved in your decision-making. You won't have to live with the consequences of accidentally angering a bunch of guards in Battahl's jail or fret over when to use the Unmaking Arrow. This one-hit-kill item still triggers an autosave as soon as it's fired, creating tension the moment you decide to draw back the bowstring, but the ability to select an earlier save robs it of its finality. If these changes were strictly tied to Dark Arisen, they wouldn't be quite as detrimental. Yet this sanding down of its intentional friction affects the base game, too.

By changing these systems, Capcom has fundamentally altered what made Dragon's Dogma 2 so fantastic. The emergent moments that happened on the road, the interplay between its systems and physics engine, and the need to deal with the consequences of your actions--all of this combined to make the base game an unpredictable and powerful adventure that stood apart from other similar games. I enjoyed its resolute design because it forced me to tackle the world on its own terms, providing a challenge that captured a rousing sense of adventure. I still thoroughly enjoyed Dark Arisen, despite these changes, but there's also this nagging feeling that something's missing.  

Norgan, for its part, has moments where that classic Dragon's Dogma abrasiveness returns. The frigid region offers a nice change of pace from the verdant forests and arid deserts explored previously, with jagged, snow-capped cliffs; frozen rivers; icy tundras; and dilapidated, long-abandoned castles. 

The hyperboreal climate doesn't just provide a visual shift, either; the weather can be both an ally and a foe. Sudden blizzards blanket the screen in heavy snowfall, obscuring your view within a few feet and taking the mini-map out of action. You also can't fast travel during these intense whiteouts, which restores the feeling that you're battling the elements just as much as the ghouls and monsters waiting to ambush you.

On the flip side, you can also use the environment to your advantage, knocking down giant overhead icicles to crush your foes, throwing and skewering them on pointy shards of ice, and rolling boulders downhill that grow larger as they pick up more and more snow. You'll appreciate the extra help when facing down demonic goatmen and abominable snowman-esque monstrosities with gnarly mouths that cover their entire torsos.   

At first glance, Norgan's map might seem fairly small, but there's a sprawling verticality both above and below the surface that hides its true density. New climbing hooks and grapple points have been introduced to help you tackle the elevation changes, while interconnected dungeons and caves reveal shortcuts amid expansive ancient ruins and the piles of loot therein. 

Speaking of which, Dark Arisen entirely reworks the game's loot system with the addition of Relics. You'll unearth this generic gear in chests and on the bodies of defeated enemies, and can then take it to one of the new vendor characters for appraisal, revealing what kind of equipment you've found, its raw stats, and, on occasion, whether it has any special attributes that enhance weapon skills or grant passive abilities. 

This creates a Monster Hunter-esque loop as you explore and defeat powerful foes to gather more Relics, before returning to the appraiser to reveal what you've found and upgrade your character. An unseen dice roll determines the type and quality, and no single piece of gear will remain in use for too long before you'll invariably find something better, but this is still a marked improvement over the base game's uninspired loot system--where I think I purchased more usable weapons and armor than I actually found. 

The base game's map has also received a similar gameplay loop of its own, with the addition of 12 repeatable dungeons known as the Lost Rite Trials. While you can easily travel back and forth between Norgan and Vermund/Battahl, these new dungeons are designed to be tackled before venturing into the snowy north, setting you up with more powerful gear to make the transition easier, with repeat visits netting more and more loot. 

Some of these dungeons are fairly straightforward (reach the end and kill everything along the way), while others rely on different mechanics or require prerequisites before you can enter. One such trial, which asks you to enter as either an Archer or Magick Archer, features rooms filled with support pillars you can shoot down to topple the terrain and wipe out groups of enemies. 

Aside from better gear, completing each Lost Rite Trial nets you an Offering Flame. These can be used at various Obelisks scattered across the frigid Norgan landscape to unlock new vocation skills and stat buffs that boost health, damage, and so on. 

The new skills are welcome, though bumping up the amount of skills you can equip at one time from four to six is a more notable improvement. Having more options at your disposal opens up the game's combat a tad more, and somewhat makes up for the lack of new vocations. As someone who's already maxed out most of the game's classes, though, I realize I might notice the dearth of new ones more than most.

One of the more surprising things about Dark Arisen is how much I enjoyed its self-contained story. Okay, maybe "enjoyed" is the wrong word, but I was definitely more invested in seeing how things would unfold than I was in the base game. It's a solid-enough vehicle for getting you from point A to B, even if it's still just remixing themes from the first game. More characters are involved, and you spend significant time with them, so there's at least something to latch onto. The writing still has little time for subtlety or nuance, but it's appropriately broody, and the story eventually leads you to some fantastic set pieces and locations. 

At over 20 hours long, Dragon's Dogma 2: Dark Arisen is the kind of hefty expansion we don't see very often. I might like this softened version of the game a little less than I did before, but even if some of its prickly nature has been smoothed over, Dark Arisen still offers a whole bunch more of what I loved about Dragon's Dogma 2 in the first place. For first-time players, and those returning with new hopes, it's a more approachable game, and I hope this helps it reach a broader audience. Whichever side of the divide you fall on, there's something here for both. That might sound blasphemous to some, and its vision isn't as unflinching as it was once before, but Dark Arisen--and Dragon's Dogma 2 on the whole--are still well worth your time and attention.
