There’s no escaping the obvious X+Y format when describing the absolutely wonderful Ambrosia Sky. That’s because it’s absolutely Prey meets PowerWash Simulator, albeit with a smaller budget. And that works for me.

Dalia is a Scarab, a sort of investigative action-type who is returning to her former colony of stations situated around the rings of Saturn, trying to recover survivors and data after the outbreak of a fungal disease called Clusterlung. Not only do these spores infect humans and take over their bodies (not turning them into zombies, but simply killing them), but they are also growing in vast arrays around space stations, ships, satellites and outposts, and you’re here to clear them up.

However, rather than simply being PowerWash In Space, Ambrosia Sky takes much of its influences from immersive sims, most especially Prey. It’s not that epic or elaborate – I don’t want to over-inflate expectations – but it lifts enough neat ideas when it comes to exploration and navigation for it to feel impactful.

This is a level-based game, each mission in a different location, meaning sections are contained rather than sprawling. However, that’s not to say they’re small, because many are absolutely enormous. There are some huge space stations with multiple hubs, networks of doors and passageways you need to figure out how to unlock, all while cleaning up the fungal masses with your handy spray gun. Two levels (one in each of the two chapters) are set in open space, as you explore enormous structures and attempt to rewire them such that you can get inside and fix vital equipment, while recovering dead people’s DNA-storing data cards for something called Project Ambrosia.

As you get deeper, the types of fungus and the ways in which you eliminate them becomes more complicated, with types themed around explosives, electricity and the like. Your spray gun has infinite water, but gets more limited options for a more powerful spray, a flame thrower, and an electricity-conducting goop, all used for clearing bases and solving puzzles in innovative ways.

All this is plenty, and just the methodical pleasure of spraying rooms fiddled with fungal structures would keep me happy, but there’s a lot more going on here too. Fungi grow fruits, and these can be harvested (if you’re careful) and used for upgrades, making things far more tactical than just aiming a fire hose and waiting. But more importantly, all those im-sim elements mean you’re also figuring out how removing the electric fungi can fix an overloaded door, but only once you’ve worked out how to get another door open by finding a hidden passage and using your tether to pull yourself up a huge tower, before… That sort of thing. And yes, a grappling tether! Automatic extra 10% added to the score.

On top of all of this is the ongoing story, which is perhaps a little too esoteric in the wrong ways in the first act, but more esoteric in all the right ways in the second. Dalia left her girlfriend Maeve when she left the Cluster some years ago, and is wracked with guilt about this decision. Maeve remained and since led the effort to fight the disease rampaging through this Saturn colony, as you’ll discover through a billion emails read on terminals as you explore. This sense of loss and grief, while still believing she made the right decision, makes Dalia a far more interesting character to play. There’s also the even more complicated relationship she had with her stepmother, Hale Volkova, a controlling but ambiguously motivated leader in the colony, in conflict with Maeve, and bitter about Dalia’s leaving.

It’s important to be clear that this isn’t a fully realised immersive sim, but something smaller, more restricted, albeit plenty long and interesting. Levels being self-contained makes this feel more piecemeal, although it does have the advantage of letting you replay them after completing their story missions, returning to 100% the clean-up and find the hidden items in hard-to-reach places. And, while I’m griping, the tether really needs a bit more work – it can be frustrating as often as it’s satisfying, and I think there needs to be a little bit more lenience on letting you dramatically swing through doorways and gaps, rather than being pernickity about catching one pixel of a window frame.

Act 2, released recently, adds a good number of new ideas, including a whole new fungal type that really affects how you approach levels, and gives the story a satisfying and enormously peculiar ending. It’s interesting how it also takes the emphasis away from cleaning and more on exploration, which I think, after doing quite so much housework in the first half, was actually rather pleasant.

There’s nothing else like it, the story is intriguing, the voice acting fantastic, and oh my goodness the art is sublime, both in levels and in the stunningly drawn still cutscenes. The fact that this is all packed into a $25 game is remarkable, and well worth your eyes.

Ace Combat 8 Is Utterly Absurd–And Better Off For It

📅 Sep 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I never knew I could be so enamored by clouds. There were times in my youth–typically sitting on a train or in the back of a car–when I would absentmindedly stare out the window at interesting cloud formations. Nowadays, phones, laptops, and handheld consoles tend to distract me from the world passing by, but in Ace Combat 8: Wings of Theve, clouds are its ever-present pièce de résistance. 

For all the high-tech jets, explosive action, and melodrama at the game's core, it's the bountiful sky and its stunning portraits of a version of our planet at varying altitudes that truly grab the eye. Flying through a rain cloud as it douses the cockpit window with droplets of water never gets old, and simply cruising through the air is almost tranquil at times. 

Yet Ace Combat 8's clouds aren't mere window dressing. From gauging your altitude by the density of the clouds around you to using them as cover to break a missile lock, Ace Combat 8 uses this technology to push the series forward in tangible ways that go beyond aesthetics.

Ace Combat 7: Skies Unknown is one of the best examples of a game singlehandedly reviving a dormant series–and earning back its goodwill after the disappointment of Assault Horizon–so Ace Combat 8 didn't have to change all that much to keep the good times rolling. It stays true to the series' arcadey dogfighting roots while implementing a number of incremental improvements, making for a slightly more dynamic and intense experience as you go from sustaining heavy G-forces to unleashing a barrage of missiles. 

The story is more prominent and also personal this time around, too, strictly focusing on the members of a single squadron amid a major conflict. Set in Ace Combat's fictional war-torn world of Strangereal, the story begins as the Republic of Sotoa invades the Federation of Central Usea (FCU), quickly occupying much of the nation and scattering its military forces.

Outmanned and outgunned, the FCU relies on propaganda to keep morale alive. This is where the eponymous Wings of Theve comes into play: a legendary fighter pilot known for taking down dozens of enemy aircraft in the fight against the invading Sotoa forces. 

Except none of the pilot’s legend is true. 

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n5irVTl0mD8

War propaganda is nothing new, of course, but this immediately reminded me of Ukraine's Ghost of Kyiv. This mythical flying ace was alleged to have shot down six Russian planes over the capital city of Kyiv back in February 2022. The Ghost's exploits quickly spread like wildfire in the media and online, but two months later, the Ukrainian air force revealed it was simply propaganda to boost morale.

It's easy to see how this might've influenced developer Bandai Namco Aces when creating Wings of Theve. But this is also an Ace Combat game, so it doesn't take long for things to get weird. Top Gun has always heavily influenced the series, so just imagine a scenario where Top Gun: Maverick opens with Tom Cruise crashing and dying, then passing his spirit on to a new pilot who adopts the Maverick callsign. 

For the rest of the movie, Tom Cruise exists as a dead guy in the new, silent protagonist's head, passing on sage wisdom and sharing his jaded thoughts on the war with a melancholy similar to the most recent Wolfenstein's B.J. Blazkowicz. Oh, and Top Gun is an anime now, too.

That is Ace Combat in a nutshell. A dead pilot narrating from inside your head might sound odd if you've never played the series before, but this kind of left-field choice is par for the course. Okay, maybe it has never gone quite this far--the Johnny Silverhand gimmick suits Cyberpunk much better--but it still fits the eccentric tone. There's no rhyme or reason to it, and the story wouldn't change all that much if it were removed entirely, yet I can't help feeling oddly gleeful that it even exists. 

Unfortunately, the basic premise doesn't hold up to much scrutiny. The pilot killed in the opening was the propagandized Wings of Theve, alleged to have shot down multiple enemies despite not opening fire on a single foe. Their famed callsign is now yours, but because this is a video game, it doesn't take long to turn fiction into fact. The Wings of Theve isn't an idea anymore; it's a real pilot who's gunning down enemies and turning the tide of war almost single-handedly, so anything interesting that might've derived from the propaganda angle is disappointingly absent.

Similarly, Ace Combat 8 lays the groundwork to comment on modern warfare in the age of social media, AI, and the mass sharing of disinformation, but all of these themes are ultimately window dressing. Social media is bad when the bad guys use it but good when the good guys do--this is the kind of social commentary you can expect.

Nuanced discussions aren't on the menu; instead, Ace Combat 8 basks in melodrama, with writing full of bold ideological statements and rousing speeches about borders, heroism, and the big blue sky. In the wrong hands, it could feel ham-fisted, but there's also a wide-eyed earnestness at play that's both endearing and optimistic. 

The new first-person perspective in cutscenes regularly features moments where you need to hit A to salute, shake hands, and so on, which veers into "Press F to pay respects" territory. Yet without Call of Duty's self-seriousness, it works in a campy sort of way. 

Humanizing touches also help the cast's pronounced personalities shine through, from Tasha Severskaya, a pink-haired civilian who's the squad's best pilot but hasn't yet pulled the trigger, to her wingman William Coster, who stays mostly silent until a mission where he learns one of the pilots you're trying to rescue used to be in his favorite rock band.

Ace Combat 8 does a good job building up your squad by delving into their unique character arcs through cutscenes and radio chatter during missions. The story has issues and is fairly by-the-numbers, but its bombast is entertaining enough, especially when it indulges in absurdity in a way only Ace Combat can. 

Where else would you face off against an ominous land battleship equipped with a railgun that can wipe out entire city blocks in an instant? Or regularly run into an evil squadron of enemy pilots that plays ominous music over the radio whenever they show up; music that gradually evolves each time they appear, eventually culminating in a pulse-racing dogfight set to some of the best video game music you'll likely ever hear.

Again, this is par for the course, whether it's a sweeping orchestral piece, a prolonged Spanish Guitar solo, or an up-tempo J-rock track. Ace Combat 8 lives up to the series' reputation for phenomenal music, adding another indispensable layer to the game's thrilling combat.

Flying through a rain cloud as it douses the cockpit window with droplets of water never gets old, and simply cruising through the air is almost tranquil at times.

Piloting its assortment of real and fictional jets is surprisingly simple, letting you get into the swing of things relatively quickly. Ace Combat 8 still has that classic arcadey Ace Combat style, but the planes feel slightly heavier than before--like there's more heft beneath the joystick--adding to the satisfying sensation that hits when you accelerate at full tilt and break through the sound barrier. 

The unmistakable crack of a sonic boom, accompanied by the distinctive vapor cone that engulfs your jet and then dissipates just as quickly as it arrives, is always exciting to pull off. Combine this with the way you can execute high-G turns, either to attempt to stay on the enemy's tail or dodge incoming missiles, and the act of soaring through the air is enjoyable in and of itself. 

Blowing up enemy aircraft is its own kind of reward, of course. Tougher enemies ensure that it's challenging to simply stay on their tail and achieve a missile lock-on, using tricks you would expect human pilots to use, such as dashing through clouds to break line-of-sight, launching flares, and pulling off cobra maneuvers to turn the hunter into the hunted. Some larger aircraft also have multiple lock-on targets, letting you pick whether you want to focus on, say, taking out their anti-aircraft guns first or destroying their propellers to send them tumbling out of the sky.   

Between knocking down buildings in the path of the aforementioned land battleship and shooting down suicidal drones to prevent them from destroying a space elevator, there's also plenty of variety from one mission to the next. After 30 years of Ace Combat, it feels like Bandai Namco Aces picked a few of their favorite scenarios from the series' past, while sprinkling in some new sortie types as well. 

One mission, for instance, has you flying an unarmed aircraft through a valley while avoiding detection from enemy radar. Others have you establish airspace dominance, follow contrails to locate massive stealth bombers, and take out coastal ground forces to protect your naval fleet's advance. There's a plethora of blood-pumping moments, although it does stumble into some pacing issues as well.

With 30 levels in total, Ace Combat 8 seems stretched at times. Some missions feel like filler, slightly nudging the story forward while tasking you with fairly basic objectives as though you're simply painting by numbers. They're not intrinsically bad, but these missions typically follow some of Ace Combat 8's more dramatic endeavors. 

I understand potentially wanting to break these levels up with more relaxed sorties to help you catch your breath, but it has the adverse effect of stalling the game's momentum. Fortunately, this isn't a problem as the campaign ramps up toward a particularly histrionic conclusion, yet I can't help but feel like it could've been a more tightly paced experience if a few missions were cut.

That said, I must commend some of these levels for being more open-ended, giving you the freedom to pick and choose your targets, which ties into Ace Combat 8's deeper pre-flight options. 

Before each mission, you can select your plane, its armaments, and a number of parts geared toward enhancing your mobility, weaponry, and other miscellaneous stats. You unlock all of this through an extensive Aircraft Tree, giving you new jets, weapons, and upgrades to play around with throughout the entirety of the game.

Some jets are tailored more for speed and dogfighting, while others excel at wiping out ground targets with bombs and land-based missile strikes. You can further improve these aspects with upgrade parts that enhance maneuverability, shorten air-to-air missile lock-on time, expand your bomb payload, and more. This isn't a rigid, universal system like before, either; each aircraft has a specific capacity for each upgrade type, making some of the more expensive jets feel much more powerful.

You can also outfit each squadmate with specific aircraft and weaponry, maybe balancing land-based attacks with air support, or fully favoring one or the other. However, I never noticed much difference in practice. The return of squad commands is more palpable, letting you order your squadmates to utilize their special weaponry when heavy targets appear, encourage them to be more aggressive, or demand that they fall back and support you. 

If you'd prefer having actual humans by your side, Ace Combat 8 also features the series' most exhaustive multiplayer suite so far. There are 100-player lobbies where you can customize your pilot avatar and wander around, visit the arcade to play Pac-Man, or head to a supply shop to obtain loot crates, among other distractions. 

There's also a battle pass--called the Ace Pass--with both free and premium tiers that unlock upgrades, consumables, and cosmetics for both your avatar and aircraft. Premium pass holders get a new fighter jet right out of the gate, plus extra progression resources that make it easier to level up and acquire better planes and upgrade parts.  

You can purchase new aircraft with an in-game currency earned by completing matches, but you're also limited by your rank, so you can't just buy the best jets right away. This seems like a good solution, but without skill-based matchmaking, players who have put in more hours--especially those who also own a premium Ace Pass--have a major advantage over everyone else. 

Ace Combat 8's multiplayer rests on two main pillars: Co-op Assault and Co-op Warfare, which let you jump into PvE or PvP matches, respectively. Co-op Assault challenges teams of two to four players to complete a shared objective, rewarding victory to the team with the highest score. 

Co-op Warfare is similar, only you can destroy players on the other team as well, racking up points by taking out computer-controlled units and real players alike. Once again, the highest score wins, but escalating scores also raise the threat level, adding a score multiplier to each kill. The higher the threat, the bigger the swings, making for some intense matches. It's just a shame the objectives are so samey, with each match focused on simply blowing up as many things as possible.  

Additionally, limited-time modes occasionally pop up in lobbies. Scramble lets a team of four go head-to-head against powerful computer-controlled squads, while Emergency Sorties task eight players with defeating iconic superweapons from previous Ace Combat games. Special PvP events also crop up from time to time, featuring four-on-four deathmatches and eight-player free-for-alls. 

Dogfights against human players are inherently fascinating, because even the best enemy AI can't possibly compare with the human mind. There's also plenty here if you'd prefer to team up with friends, but I can't see the player base sustaining for too long after the honeymoon period ends. The missions in Co-op Assault and Co-op Warfare devolve into tedium after three or four matches, and the unbalanced nature of the whole thing is frustrating.

Despite the multiplayer falling short, Ace Combat 8's single-player adventure is still worth the price of admission alone. The story could've been stronger--more introspective--but is entertaining regardless, and while there are some pacing issues with filler missions and some uneven momentum, much of the game is excellent, providing the series' signature blend of melodrama, stirring music, and thrilling aerial combat. Ace Combat 8: Wings of Theve picks up where Ace Combat 7 left off, underlining that the series is well and truly back to its best.

Alicia aims to be the cool aunt to her niece Dottie. One of the aspects of being the cool aunt is fighting weird otherworldly creatures that both naturally find themselves attuned to see. The creatures are just kinda weird scary pests, like the scorpions that invade my home during the summer, so there’s no serious problems.

But then, something weird and unfortunate happens. Dottie’s hometown of Daybreak suddenly vanishes from the outside world. Alicia, who was called before the town disappeared, pokes into the strange otherworld the town became adjacent to. In the new world, deadlier otherworld creatures run rampant, dancing to the tune of Inner Evocations responsible the town being spirited away.

Alicia allies with the Outer Evocation Elothu, who bestows greater power on Alicia so that she may be able to rescue Dottie. Allying with some other humans blessed by their own Outer Evocations, Alicia goes on to put an end to the weird and unfortunate things happening.

Weird and Unfortunate Things Are Happening is a 2020 game by unity, who went on to publish the game on Steam under Sapphire Dream Games. Hey, remember the Misaos stuff from a long time ago? The game was actually the winner of the 2020 iteration, and the dev’s previous game, Luxaren Azure, is mentioned in that post. Weird Things has been in the back of my mind for a loooong time, so it was a blast when I spun it for my backlog bingo.

(Also god I need to fix that post up, RPG Maker net going offline for a while really messed with things…)

The game starts with Alicia delving into a distorted modern day small town. There’s some goofy bits of graffiti and a few friendly monsters, but it’s hardly an “Earthbound inspired game about depression” like internet losers talk about. Even with the bouts of humor that can be found, the game is a miserable cosmic horror story. The character designs are cute, but the horror isn’t. The friendly Outer Evocations contrast strongly with the Inner Evocations, who eagerly distort and kill humans and urge the lesser apparitions to do the same in pursuit of a goal that’s a huge spoiler. Human bosses make classic devil deals with them, and they wind up distorting into nightmarish abominations when their contractors find their work inadequate. There’s very little downplaying the effects the otherworldly had on Daybreak; like, even with part of the violence abstracted, the game is pretty open about the survivors of the local school being in the single digits. I was slow to play through this game because I’m generally not in the right headspace for it, but when my mood was good, I was loving this shit.

But why stress about the horrors? Why not beat them to death? We love beating things to death, folks.

Weird Things is a game of classic turn-based action with four core characters. Alicia is a fine middle of the road character that dabbles in different fields, but when it came down to it, I preferred building her as an offensive tank. If you got a move that attracts and counters physical damage, you gotta be a tank. It feels fitting for her to be the tank storywise, as the events of the story’s dependent on her wanting to shoulder the burdens of the quest to protect Dottie.

The second character Miriam is an aggressive woman who was put out of work before getting dragged into otherworldly hell, so she fights with guns like a true angry hot-blooded American. A fun thing is that she could fire unique bullets that are much stronger, but they’re limited in number. When a boss battle’s going south, I absolutely have Miriam treating things like a shooting gallery with her special bullets. Her having moves that poison people in a game where poison’s actually a pretty reliable damage dealer makes her a truly respectable American.

As a teacher that truly cares for his students and the people around him, Lamar has direct heals to work alongside Alicia’s own and can apply shields against damage types and a shield that nullifies status ailments. When he isn’t helping everyone, he can really tear things up with magic, as he’s definitely a brilliant man outside of his teaching job, and as we all know, smart characters in RPGs are the best wizards. An interesting thing about using Lamar is that his basic skills are different depending on which grimoire he has equipped, so it may be worth holding on to grimoires if you want to use the skills connected to them instead of selling them off to the Normal merchant.

Meanwhile, we have Dottie, who was targeted by the Inner Evocations for her unique magic potential. While the other characters have elemental specialties, Dottie has the strongest variety in who she can target. Dottie also has a unique skill that charges up between turns that carries after battle, and I actually thank the developer for making it and advising me on how to replicate it. And meanwhile, she can also gift her mana to other characters, becoming a magic battery of her own free will as opposed to being forced into that role.

Dottie can also sacrifice chess pieces for party buffs, I guess, but I’ll be real, I kept forgetting she could do that.

The characters all have their own tech trees with fresh new skills and passives to buy into. Advancing the tech tree requires obols, a currency you get from fighting enemies and finding them on the field. Scattering stuff to advance the tech tree on the map lends a stronger incentive to explore around, so I wound up enjoying the tech tree stuff. A fun thing to find when exploring around are small otherworld pockets that offer a bunch of treasure and obols in what feels like a Yume Nikki fangame. Weird Things really is a game that tries to embody RPG Maker culture.

There’s a strong variety of enemies to fight, from classic RPG fare, stuff based on mythological horrors, references to horror media, abstract horrors, and robots (aka the modern day horror). I played the game on the second hardest difficulty, which was a pretty healthy challenge. Just enough throat cutting action to be engaging while not being too much to be alienating. The game is pretty healthy about throwing healing resources at you, so as long as you’re good about searching the map for stuff and conserving resources, I feel that Weird Things makes for a tough but fair ride.

I was comfortable at my difficulty. However, I wound up turning the difficulty down toward the end of the game. It wasn’t because I found things too hard, but because… I just wanted the game to end.

That takes me on one aspect I feel mixed on: the game’s length. Weird and Unfortunate Things Are Happening is surprisingly lengthy, which you probably won’t see as a bad thing. It’s just that it sincerely feels that there are multiple good end points. After fighting someone what could have been the final boss, the game kept going. I was like, alright, makes sense, the player got Dottie just earlier, you should spend more time using her.

A fairly lengthy dungeon follows, and a loose end in the story shows up to fight, and you’re probably thinking, “oh, THIS is the final boss!” And… no, it keeps going. While the further content justifies itself, it keeps feeling like the game should have ended earlier. With how the setting and goals of the game shifts after the first point I thought the game would end, I can’t help but feel like another game was stitched onto this one. It’s not that I was straight up bored, because interesting things WERE happening… but man, things really could have wrapped up earlier. And even then, there’s a bonus dungeon lying around…

Personally, I think if the game was going to have more content, it should be more character stuff with the main cast. While there’s plenty of banter, most character building happens in the arc they’re introduced in. There is a segment of the late game that provides flashbacks to the lives of the characters that I thought was nice, but I ended up wishing there were more moments like that, especially regarding characters introduced in the flashbacks. The main characters are fun, but I wish there was more to them. Like I dunno, maybe the endgame mini-dungeons could have been themed after the characters and given them more story moments that I would have been more invested in instead of just being… more dungeons. Like, forget five mini-dungeons, let’s have four mini-dungeons that give better glimpses into the characters.

And while the Outer Evocations feel like more could have been done with them, it makes sense why they’re content to just be sponsors. Still though, I think they paled in comparison to the Inners, who feel like they have more active presence than the Outers, even if some may feel like cartoon villains.

However, despite my misgivings, I think Weird and Unfortunate Things Are Happening is a damn great game overall that I think more people should play. Hell, there’s very little to stop you from playing it – it’s completely free. And honestly, it’s a crime that it’s free. I may be ??? at the amount of Content in the game, but the fact that there’s this much stuff available for free is bonkers. It’s especially admirable when you consider all the tilesets in the game, as the game really does try to make every location distinct. I may complain about those endgame mini-dungeons, but them having unique designs is something to appreciate.

If you want to kill time with an indie RPG and don’t got the cash to burn, this is definitely one to check out. I personally think things should have been tighter, but overall, I think Weird and Unfortunate Things Are Happening was a good time.

my steam personalized calendar: the rest of September

Hey y’all, it’s the start of a new month! Last month I shared stuff that was presented to me by Steam’s personalized calendar, and I think I’ll commit to making this a regular thing. So, here are the indie games coming out in September that Steam thought I should know about. Again, this is stuff that Steam thought I’d personally vibe with, so if there’s a game that you feel is missing, well, maybe that game just didn’t fit my good vibrations. And again, I’ll be omitting anything that uses AI. Fuck you.

On the 8th, we have Thousand Hells: The Underworld Heists, developed by A Sharp and published by Kitfox Games. Billing itself as a tactical narrative game, you assemble a team that’d be capable of descending the underworld and navigating the choices presented to you. With lots of choices and characters , Thousand Hells promises a lot of replayability.

A Sharp has plenty of experience with making this kind of game with their Six Ages series. The series grew out of King of Dragon Pass, which was originally released in 1999 and found more success with its re-releases. Thousand Hells looks to be a fresh start with a whole new mythology. Personally, this is definitely something in my wheelhouse.

Releasing on Early Access the same day, we have Randwerk’s Wanderburg. It’s a roguelike where you’re out to conquer the surrounding land. But you’re not some wandering hero or a normal army, no. Instead, you’re controlling a mobile fortress like a mech, consuming resources and building yourself up to fight armies and their own mechanized strongholds.

It seems like a neat idea for a roguelike, speaking as someone not as in love with roguelikes as I used to be. I don’t know how Wanderburg will end up, but it does call for community feedback during the Early Access period, so it may end up being something super good when it leaves Early Access.

Speaking of Early Access: on the 9th, Iron Gate AB’s Valheim finally exits its own Early Access. Originally released back in 2021, Valheim is a co-op survival game of vikings roughing it out in a cruel world. Valheim may be one of Early Access’ most successful works, boasting of hundreds of thousands of reviews on Steam with plenty of attention online. It may be a bit high-profile for this blog, but it’d be silly to not acknowledge this game.

I actually have a copy of Valheim myself that I last played near when it originally launched. I don’t think it clicked with me, but comparing to what I remember how the game was to how it is now, it’s astonishing how much has changed. I’m not sure if I’ll ever delve back in myself, but Valheim undeniably’s done great for itself.

But now, we gotta acknowledge another big game. Maybe the series isn’t big in the public consciousness, but it’s big when it comes to the sphere of indie games in general. Whether you’re a fan of bullet hell games, video game music, yuri, or a mix of that stuff, you have a home with ZUN’s Touhou project. Touhou’s created a sub-culture with a strong series of games and a commercial fangame culture building off its mythology. I’ve seen some Deltarune fans scoff at the more explicit Touhou references in the game, and I get it, y’all hate the inspirations of the game you love so much; y’all love jacking off to the shmup action of Undertale/Deltarune‘s combat system because you hate turn-based RPGs, but hate the idea of engaging with shmups outside of these games, get out of here.

And for the appropriate date of 9/9, a remake of the sixth game, Embodiment of the Scarlet Devil, arrives. A scarlet mist has descended on the land of Gensokyo, and local shrine maiden Reimu and magician Marisa sets out to resolve the supernatural incident in a bullet hell adventure. With remastered graphics, music, and a sort of casual mode where you can play with unlimited lives, Embodiment of Scarlet Devil is making a bold return for modern audiences.

Scarlet Devil may be the entry that’s most familiar in the overall gaming sphere, being the first entry that released on Windows. You may know the song UN Owen Was Her, which incidentally also got me into Agatha Christie’s And Then There Were None – both good stuff. I wish I could play this, but I just don’t have the reflexes for shmups like I used to have and I’d get overstimulated hard by Touhou’s action and music. But for the shmup and/or yuri lovers out there, this is definitely the game for you.

Hopping on Early Access on the 10th is Shroom and Gloom, developed by Team Lazerbeam and published by Devolver Digital. There’s a dungeon full of mushroom monsters that you’ll be fighting through with some deckbuilding action… though, you can simply opt to eat the monsters too like you’re on some Dungeon Meshi shit.

Shroom and Gloom differentiates itself with first-person exploration and a separate deck dedicated to the exploration layer of the game. Being able to explore the setting seems nice, especially with its strong hand-drawn aesthetic. There seems to be a stronger focus on upgrading cards as your method of getting stronger instead of gathering passives, which seems like a shift from the normal flow for deckbuilder roguelikes.

Something escaping Early Access on the 10th is Andrei Fomin’s Effulgence RPG, which originally released in December of last year. If you have a love of ASCII art, this is a game for you, as the game is defined with a world of 3D models made up of ASCII characters. You guide your party on an adventure, defeating the surreal ASCII grotesqueries and taking their characters to a printer where you could reform them into weapons and abilities to help you further.

Effulgence has a really cool aesthetic, and I love the whole thing of recycling the characters that made up the things you beat into something new. It’s kinda a more light hearted contrast to being able to straight up devour your enemies in Shroom and Gloom.

Anyway, let’s head into horror. Also releasing on the 10th is Welcome to Elderfield, developed by Chris Cote and published by Kwalee. The town of Elderfield presents a cozy horror RPG experience, where you’re managing a farm in a place haunted by eldritch horrors. There’s in-depth RPG combat amidst your escapades of farm management and relationship building, and while you can turn down the difficulty of that to have a truly cozy time… you’re already dealing with the Horrors, so where’s the fun in that?

I know I talk a lot on this blog about not liking cozy games because I have a power in misery mindset, but the cozy stuff in this game feels like it’d work for me because it makes for a good contrast with the game’s other elements. One of the most exciting areas of new, lesser-known gaming right now is in the so-called “incremental” space. These are games that exist in the aftermath of the clicker genre, or “idle games”, a bizarro (and often excellent) world of games about unlocking new skills in order to unlock new skills, all with the ultimate goal of watching numbers go up. As with most genres that strip away many layers of complexity, there will always be a following generation of developers who want to slowly introduce them back, and that’s how you get the incremental game.

Ignoble is such a brilliant example of this. Idle games are known for their enormous skill trees, with squillions of unlocks that dramatically improve your abilities, and developers are now applying this concept to so many other game types. Some of my favourites have included Scritchy Scratchy and it’s daft lottery ticket format, Fortune Mill and its fairground of minigames, and the very cutesy Shelldiver, but none feel as complete and involved as Ignoble, a game about killing absolutely everything, a lot.

It’s an auto-battler, in the sense that there’s no attack button but rather you hit the screens full of enemies by standing close to them. In fact, standing close to enemies is utterly essential, as your health rapidly depletes if you’re ever not immediately next to your foes, which becomes tricky when they’re about to hit you very hard. Each screen has a string of targets to meet, which is to say enemies to kill, which unlocks new events and eventually a boss fight, which when beaten brings you to the next area. And you’ll die an awful lot.

But that’s a good thing, because as you played you also picked up a raft of currencies, including macabre delights like blood, bones, as well as various liquids and jewels, which can then be spent in the skill tree. There are also cards that are rolled for after a run that can be placed in a growing number of slots to add bonuses, and then it’s back in for another go, this time more powerful and better-equipped.

Except, rather brilliantly, the earliest stages of the game don’t stay the same. Each time you defeat a boss, or clear a milestone, the enemies start to change, newer and more powerful ones arrive, and the challenges in a level alter, ensuring you don’t get frustrated by repeating early grinds. At the same time, you get so powerful that the earliest bosses become trivial, so you always feel like you’re making massive progress.

At the point I’ve reached, I’ve unlocked (and in many cases fully levelled up) 105 different skills on the skill tree, and am now double-jumping around with a whirling volley of blades spinning around me, as I cast spells that bring down deathly spikes, while tentacles reach out from my body and slaughter farther enemies… Which is really quite something compared to the trudging guy who slowly mooched about punching enemies when things started.

That sense of constant advancing progress is the compelling key to the incremental genre, and it’s so incredibly effectively executed in Ignoble. This is boosted by fantastic pixel artwork, excellent enemy design and behaviour, wonderful squelchy sound, and an evocative rocky soundtrack. Movement, especially as you unlock all its various aspects, feels slick and fair, and enemies flag their attacks in a neat way.

And there are many other elements I’ve not mentioned, like the way cards are sacrificed, or the excellent way the game changes the shape of the frame throughout, nor the intriguing ways in which bosses are fought. It keeps revealing new aspects to me, and I feel sure it has more surprises to come. And all this for less than a fiver!

I love the way these genres evolve so inventively, and Ignoble is such a brilliant example of how. It so smartly combines the rapid incremental progress with the auto-battling made so popular by Vampire Survivors, and plays like a deeply rewarding roguelite. It’s also just a very satisfying experience in its own right, and splendidly gory with it.

I should probably be a bit embarrassed about admitting quite how long it’s taken me to complete the first of It Consumes‘ three stages. On its lowest difficulty. But I’ve had so much fun not managing it.

There are perhaps a few too many games that make their primary feature darkness, but It Consumes makes this fascinating all over again, turning light into your most precious resource. The game takes place in a pitch-black arena, lit (at first) only by the slow pulses of red light emitted from a heart you clutch in your left hand. Sending out a growing circle of vision around you, this lights up (in eerie red glows) the enemies floating toward you, such that you can blast them into oblivion with your first-person weaponry and spells. But those pulses of light don’t last long, and the enemies are unrelenting, and the reality is you’re going to die pretty quickly.

However, everything you’re doing in It Consumes is working toward unlocking something new. In your attempts at surviving this first, lengthy ordeal, every pulse emitted, shot fired, spell cast and enemy killed is chipping away at a meter that will give you access to new tactics, weapons, secondary fires and most importantly of all, ways to emit your pulses of light. And on top of that, you’re also scoring points that can be spent on a growing list of bonus abilities that tweak your next run hopefully to your advantage. It’s all about chipping away at these targets, progressing to discover your perfect combination of arms and lighting, and seeing how much farther you can reach in each new attempt.

After the Heart pulser, you get the Clock, a diamond-shaped clockwork device that automatically emits pulses of light, just so long as you keep it wound up. Winding it is done by holding down Space, and you can do that at the same time as using your mouse buttons to fire at enemies and hitting Shift to dash from attacks, but there’s a brutal delay before the pulses start emitting again. It’s all about incredibly careful timing, or indeed figuring out a way to play in the dark. The latter proves essential, as getting enough kills this way is part of unlocking the next pulser, the throwable orb…

Then there’s your Primary Fire, which starts with a rapid-fire assault of low-damage blasts, but can soon be replaced by Single Shot, more accurate, reasonably powerful attacks, but fired one at a time. Complete enough of the tasks and you’ll find out what comes after that. These are joined by Secondary Fire, more powerful and interesting attacks that are only temporarily available once you’ve shot a silver crystal in a level (and in far more complicated ways as you unlock further features), and again, these gradually unlock new forms that dramatically alter your approach. Oh, and then there are Light and Heavy spells, before you even start thinking about your list of equipped Perks.

Then there’s a third screen of unlocks that make this already incredibly hard game harder, but in ways that perhaps could be to your advantage? They’ll certainly mean you earn more for your efforts. And these pages clicked through, you’re right back in for another attempt. And another. And another. And another.

It’s all gloriously compelling, and the red-and-black theme leans in hard to the demonic nature of your enemies and the foreboding atmosphere. In a game with so much darkness, sound is essential, and It Consumes nails this. Enemies rather generously growl or holler just before attacks, which makes things far scarier, but also much more manageable.

And manageable is key: This is a game about remembering to do stuff! In any run you’ve got to think about remembering to pulse every few seconds, all while being distracted by constant barrages of enemies, but not forgetting you have those two spells on hard to help, and then to dash to avoid exploding foes (while considering your stamina), and then among all this hitting E to level up each time you gather enough XP. My cursor keys are weeping. They’ve taken such a hammering over the last week, as I’ve blasted my way through splendid little arcade game Kaz (from Kalinarm, the team that brought us the incredible Linkito). The deceptively simple-sounding concept of moving a character around a small field of tiles with your cursors (or WASD) to hit monsters and avoid traps unfurls into an incredibly tactical sprawl of dozens of characters, special moves, upgrades and even cursor-cast spells. The frenetic action is always about chasing a high score, while hitting goals to unlock coins to buy yet more ways to play. And every aspect of this is gorgeously, explosively presented with so much variety in art, sound and music.

Things of course begin reasonably simply. You have one character, Kaz herself, and a grid of four tiles in which to rapidly fling yourself around. Enemies appear in three different forms – regular, shielded, and fugitives – and are “attacked” by moving into their tile. Regular and fugitive enemies are beaten by simply moving over, while shielded enemies require an extra tap first. And at first, that’s really it.

Each level starts at 15 seconds long and has a required minimum score in order to proceed to the next. Miss a target and it’s game over, but hopefully you’ll have unlocked some new gubbins in the process. Get far enough in and traps will start to appear, their imminent spawn indicated by a red X, and if touched while briefly appearing will apply curse to your character. After each level, you get to choose from either one of three upgrades, or if you’ve filled your curse meter, one of three debuffs. There are 108 of these total to unlock, with enormous variety in the changes they make.

You might get Spawn Swarm, which fills every tile in the ever-larger grids with enemies at a random point in the round, offering a great chance to scoop up a big score. If you then pick Inflation, that makes each enemy worth more points, so those two combine extremely well. Or you might want Starchaser that makes a star appear on a tile for a very brief window that if grabbed hands you a big pile of points. But Golden Shield makes the massive difference of giving you points when breaking a shield as well as hitting the enemy underneath. Perhaps you’ll want Mole, which adds an extra tile to your grid with the first wall you bash. Oh, and possibly the most important: Jump. This gives you the ability to skip gaps between tiles and hugely frees up your movement. And on and on and on. Combining these in specific ways eventually leads to an incredibly tactical approach.

The entire time I’ve played I’ve found myself in the midpoint of extraordinary skill and just hammering the buttons like a child (or me) playing Mortal Kombat. There are always quests you’ve nearly completed, giving you a reason for just one more go, and that’s even more tempting when you can pick from one of the many characters you’ve unlocked to keep adding variety.

It’s super-impressive, deceptively complex, and immediately fun.

I’m in a quandary. I update Buried Treasure far more infrequently than I intend, thanks to so many potential games falling through, an endless supply of crises, and poor time management. If not, and here’s the nuance, this specific game – while very dark and sometimes upsetting – is not accurately depicting real-world events but rather basing a story of cosmic horror upon them. I played it without knowing about any of the historical events, and (with some caveats) thought it was genuinely excellent. You can too. But I also can’t in good conscience not raise it. It’s New York, it’s 1927, and bereaved investigative journalist Richard Pickman is following leads on a story about stolen auto-parts, when he stumbles upon the location of a missing child. There, he sees the child’s dead body attacked by some sort of monstrous creature, and immediately becomes drawn into an awful world behind reality’s veil, in true Lovecraftian style. A combination of choice-driven text adventure and light point-and-click elements, illustrated with stunning art, makes for an excellent medium for conveying the creeping sense of awful that surrounds Pickman after he becomes embroiled in the the murder of Billy Gaffney.

On top of some really fantastic writing (made even more impressive considering this is created by an Italian team), and the 900 pieces of (often very gruesome) hand-drawn art, Omen Exitio Hunger also features some of the most extraordinary sound effects I’ve ever heard. You also have, at least in the early parts of the game, some incredibly significant choices to make. Decisions that really do affect how you experience the story, the insight you gain into the eldritch mysteries, and the people you get to know properly. This is certainly something that occurs more infrequently the farther you get, with binary choices often replaced by just clicking through all the available locations and characters, and that’s definitely a shame, but it’s also a huge ask.

The other issue that occurs later on is one that besets Lovecraft’s own stories and many of the derivative works: the Dreamlands. Like too often, once Pickman ends up here – as good as the writing, art and sound remain – it’s harder to feel like it matters. It’s only superficially real, and when time and encounters don’t really contain any meaning, I just want it over and to be back into the concrete concrete city. I had the same problem with the extremely good Malevolent podcast, but perhaps other people have very different tolerance for such things.

So yes, this a very dark game. Omen Exitio Hunger (OEH) is not only themed around the grisly murder of a child, but in a move I definitely find very uncomfortable, names that child the same as a real missing four-year-old who disappeared in 1927. That’s because the story is loosely based around the true story of Albert Fish, a truly horrific serial killer, paedophile and cannibal who became so legendary as to gain multiple nicknames like The Gray Man, The Werewolf of Wysteria, the Brooklyn Vampire, and as is used in OEH, The Boogey Man. I had originally linked the Wikipedia page about him, but after having felt genuinely ill from reading it, I’m deliberately adding an extra step between you and doing the same.

OEH fictionalizes absolutely everything else surrounding the story, including the true nature of Fish (who has an entirely different name here), so I just cannot fathom why it would leave William Gaffney’s name and death the same. It seems such a deeply horrible choice, because he was a real four-year-old, and he disappeared in the same circumstances, wearing the same clothes, and killed in the same truly horrific way. So what do I do with that? Did I find this game repellent as a result? Well, no, not as I played it. Have I thus cursed this game to being repellent for everyone else by writing this? I could hardly not mention it, and the game’s store page goes to lengths to establish its true inspiration. So should I have not written about it at all? But it’s a truly superb game. And here’s the other thing: you’re an adult who can make their own choices.

I very much love the idea of games that find unexplained mysteries from the past and then overlay them with fantastical explanations. There are so many options for doing this, and viewed purely with macabre fascination, Albert Fish would seem to fit the bill. He was truly one of the most inexplicably horrendous and fundamentally broken human beings I’ve ever read about, and his being the incarnation of some cosmic evil would make a lot of sense. But the kid? I didn’t need the same kid. I wish I’d never found out.

So do with this what you will. Genuinely, I would love to see what developers Tiny Bull Studios do next, given the astonishing talent on display here. They’re the team behind Don’t Nod’s The Lonesome Guild, after all. This is their second game in this series, the first – Omen Exitio: Plague – is similar but with more RPG elements, is historically rooted but seemingly entirely fictional. Personally, I’d love it if their third were too.

Well, assuming that the slayer is who he says he is with a clear mind, anyhow.

It ain’t a social deduction game without evil lurking around. Evil characters disguise themselves as an ordinary villager and tell lies. It starts simple with minions who are just plain liars. Then there’s the witch that prevents you from looking at the last card and a chancellor that forces an outcast in the field to muddy the waters. Your objective? Execute all evil. Are you truly the good guy when execution’s your main objective and you seem to be doing it on behalf of a demon? (shrug)

But things start to get harder when corruption is introduced. You can’t just go killing people for the crime of lying, no, they may be lying because they may be corrupted. If a corruption causing character has a chance of joining the game, you have to be more careful. Executing a knight initially seems like a safe gambit because they only get executed if it’s actually an evil disguise, but if a legitimate knight is corrupted, you’ll accidentally kill a perfectly good guy. So not only will you be figuring out lies, but you also have to figure out if the liar actually is an evil person. Is this a person corrupted by the poisoner or a plague doctor? Is the liar just a disguised drunk who isn’t even corrupted and just lies for the bit? Executing too many innocent people removes a life and starts you over with a new village. Lose all your lives and the run ends.

You’re looking through his files, and you find a curious program your uncle wanted you to look at: Desktop Explorer.

Desktop Explorer is a point-and-click mystery adventure game framed through a 90’s computer. It’s certainly something to play in the current age of software that’s kinda hell. There’s a Clippy style mascot character named Pizarro around to give tips and I’ll be honest, I wholeheartedly welcome Pizarro in this age of AI chatbots that exist to jack you off while being extremely unhelpful. And besides, it’s nice to have a friendly face around as the game starts to delve into darker territory.

The game starts out with simple puzzles with a narrative of exploring a cave. As you play on, you unlock journals from your uncle. You uncover the unfolding narrative of his declining health with amnesia, and his angst with a horrible loss that he wishes he can forget. The adventure presented through the Desktop Explorer files becomes more personal and unsettling as you do stuff like dig into source code of text and find hidden files. Though you don’t get to see the internet, it at all feels like if Hypnospace Outlaw went more into horror, feeling a bit like the stuff by Tony Domenico. Did Guppy’s uncle want to pass his stories on through his Desktop Explorer stuff… or is there something else at work?

Desktop Explorer is a neat little adventure game, and I feel a sorta kinship with the background narrative as someone that also has a neurodegenerative disease. I really like what’s already there, and the little trailer that plays at the end certainly hints at more to come with the computer framing.

The last game for this that I’m gonna yap about is Blood Dungeon, by Messhof. Messhof’s most known for the Nidhogg games and I had fun playing those. While their game Wheel World passed me by, Messhof’s taking a new step with this demo I was really into.

You’ve been sentenced to the Blood Dungeon, and the only way to survive is through autoshooting platformer action. The 2D platforming aspect of the game adds another dimension to action typical of “bullet heaven” stuff, forcing you to navigate through a maze of platforms as enemies threaten to close in. Enemies coming from below are more threatening, as you can’t just “move up.” Movement is very fluid, as you can climb up walls and hang onto the ceiling, so you aren’t exactly fully restricted in the 2D platformer space. Granted, I’ve only really played Vampire Survivors and Brotato for this type of game, but a game that’s asking for more movement than just walking makes for a more engaging experience.

Harsher waves spawn as time goes on, encouraging you to stay moving. Eventually, a boss spawns. All of the ones in the demo seems to be a giant serpentine creature that likes to dive toward you. I wonder… will the titular Nidhogg make its appearance in the future?

Progression is standard for bullet heavens. Collect blood to level up for more weapons or level up perks. Get bones based off your final score to level up in the metagame. Unlock more stuff to get for level ups and more characters.

Characters are simple yet bright against the darker background. The music is energetic stuff that I was really into. It’s a taste of colorful ultraviolence, and in offering it in a way that’s easy to play for my fucked hands while giving you more to do than just walking around, I’m all for this. And so, I happily got Blood Dungeon on my wishlist.

Uncle Lee’s Cookbook: Five Recipes For Disaster

📅 May 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

PC, Mac, Linux

At first glance you’d assume that Uncle Lee’s Cookbook is a fairly traditional point-and-click adventure. Aesthetically it’s clearly designed to look a lot like early-’90s LucasArts, and mechanically you play it with a mouse cursor, inventory and so on. But this five-chapter game is very subtly something else. It’s–well four fifths of it at least–more of a puzzle game played as a PnC adventure. And that’s something that works splendidly well. (The other fifth? That is a traditional adventure game!)

Uncle Lee is, but for the prologue, not the player character. Instead that’s Ines, his niece whom he raised, whose life seems to be mostly made up of fixing the reality-breaking mistakes her maverick scientist uncle continuously makes. This means for four out of the five chapters in Uncle Lee’s Cookbook, you’re in these fantastic little vignettes, almost like bottle-episode adventures in which you need to manipulate whichever aspect of the laws of physics Lee has broken in order to put things as close to right as is available.

In the first chapter, for instance, Uncle Lee wants some ice cream from the massive 3D printer he’s installed in (the adult) Ines’ bedroom. However, he’s also fractured reality at a quantum level such that all is in quantum flux until he personally observes it. This means that every choice Ines makes from her perspective is unmade from Lee’s, and as such when he looks at you those quantum positions collapse into the least helpful form. In other words, if you go into the next room and pick up a baseball, on walking into the kitchen Lee’s glance means you now didn’t. Except, the issue is to fix this you need to bring him three specific items, each of which is frustrated by a puzzle before you can find it. And if he sees you, you didn’t actually do any of that.

The second chapter jumps into the past, rendered in GameBoy green, and Uncle Lee has both accidentally turned himself into an electric eel and rendered the reality of your home into a Moebius strip. In the third you’re time travelling entirely within the confines of a single campervan. And in the fifth it’s about literally rewriting the rules of reality with a magic book. And the fourth? That’s a completely separate, almost Lee-free story about Ines trying to rid a small town of its ghosts so she can have piece and quiet in Lee’s childhood home to write a book, with multiple locations, characters, puzzles and a suite of magic powers!

The whole thing lasts three or four hours total, and is absolutely packed with incredibly smart ideas and very rewarding puzzles. There’s also a meticulous in-game hint system from a peculiar collection of hooded figures, and a lots of gentle knowing gags. In the main I’m strongly against Monkey Island references and fourth-wall breaking references to being a game in point-and-click adventures, due to how ubiquitous and self-aggrandising they so often are. But not so here–they’re sweet, and one recurring joke where characters inform you “there’s no gold in this game” got a chuckle out of me. Maybe I’m getting soft.

It’s superbly drawn and animated, and quite extraordinarily in a self-made engine from one of the three developers behind the game. (I’m really interested to know the decision behind that instead of going with Adventure Game Studio.) It’s also fully voiced, with the fantastic Maganda Marie as Ines, Nicholas Camm as Uncle Lee, and the beloved Sally Beaumont in a bunch of roles.

I had a lovely time with this especially interesting approach to the genre. Had it just been chapter 4 alone as a short adventure I’d have still been recommending this, but that it has the other four excellent conceits around it makes it into something novel and delightful.
