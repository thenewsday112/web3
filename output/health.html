Life-Sim Meets Otherworldly, Encroaching Horror in Village In The Shade

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Cozy life-sim games where you get to retreat to a rustic village for a relaxing, self-sufficient life on a farmstead are hardly in short supply--and, with so many stellar entries in this genre out there, a game following this formula needs a really good hook to stand out. Village in the Shade, a new release from NIS America on Switch, Switch 2, PlayStation 5, and PC, certainly looks the part of a cheerful, cozy farming game. But, as the phrase goes, looks can be deceiving--and, in this case, that deception runs deep. 

You can tell something is off right from the tutorial, which sees you chasing after an escaped chicken, only to discover something rather distressing. What happens next only leaves you with more questions and some highly unsettling imagery. Somehow, after all this, your player character emerges, stumbling into Kagatsu Village in urgent need of care. 

The Kagatsu villagers seem very attentive at first, but it quickly becomes clear that the people in charge aren't entirely altruistic. It feels like they see you less as a suffering victim and more as an engine to reap profits. You're thrown onto dirty farmland overrun with debris--which has been oh-so-kindly leased to you--and forced to sleep in a run-down shack that looks like it came from a match-3 mobile game ad. If you want to stay with the villagers, you're told, you will need to become one of them. And you'll have to work for it. Nobody will so much as speak to you until then. 

When you finally raise your first, scant batch of turnips--and offer them in a ritual sacrifice ceremony--you're made an official Kagatsu citizen. But then come the rules. The rules must be followed. They're strange. Arbitrary. Controlling. Almost cult-like. "Do not expose another person's secrets." "Do not search for what was lost." "Do not approach strangers." And, the one most commonly emphasized: "Do not go outside after 11 PM."

But the people here seem nice enough, now that they're actually talking to you. Rokkaku's a single parent who runs the local restaurant and cares deeply for his adopted niece Yoh. Hasumi might not have a medical license, but she does seem to care deeply about everyone's health. Sazanka runs the general store and wants to provide the tools for your success. Kisuke might be crass and grumpy, but you can see that the builder has a good heart underneath it all. They'll teach you how to fish, how to set up animal pens, how to hunt for wild game, and how to build a variety of tools. They all seem like a pleasant bunch, even if they can be standoffish at first. (Well, except that sleazeball Konno. Guy has "manipulative crypto bro" written all over him.)

And yet, it feels like everyone's hiding something. You catch glimpses of strange behavior, snippets of conversations likely not meant for your ears. There are noises at night. People knocking on your door at ungodly hours, screaming incoherently. Oh, no, it's just Yoh; she's lonely and wants to sleep with the baby chick she adopted that lives in your pen. No harm will come of that, right?

Eventually, circumstance forces you to break that most sacrosanct of rules, and you step out during midnight. It's here that you come face-to-face with otherworldly abominations, chasing you, obstructing your vision, making the haptics on your controller go absolutely wild. You've seen something--many things, actually--that shouldn't have been seen by humans. You suspected it, expected it, and yet it’s still no less disturbing.

You begin second-guessing the motivations for everything. Submitting your produce at a town gathering for a public evaluation contest seems innocuous enough, but it happens mere days after you've been asked to either tithe or submit to a blood-gathering "medical exam" for reasons unknown. You can't help but wonder if hunting some animals might actually be an act of mercy, given what you know is lurking out there. And there are all of those areas that are conveniently, conspicuously blocked off. Sure, you’re going to open them as you progress, but what kind of progress do you have to make? 

This is the reality of Kagatsu: a place that hides horrors beneath deceptively warm and inviting coziness. The graphics are beautifully drawn in a 2D, painterly style that's immediately appealing. The day-to-day life-sim gameplay is easy to pick up, and it's satisfying to build your farmstead enterprise from nothing. But even in its brightest moments--when you raise a bumper crop, finally craft that new setup that lets you make you make a new kind of foodstuff to sell, or help the townsfolk with their errands or give them thoughtful gifts--there's always that creeping, pitch-black shadow welling underneath. 

I've been enthralled by Village in the Shade since I picked it up. It weaves its themes of deception and unease into a routine, leaving you in a bizarre emotional state of feeling both comfortable and on-edge. The contrast between the familiar warmth of a life-sim game and a hideous, horrifying underbelly is enthralling, and I find myself both eagerly anticipating and dreading every new development in my cozy town life. If you’re looking for a different kind of life-sim--or a different kind of horror game--you’d do well to take up your tools and join the village.

Next Fest October 2026

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

As usual it’s taken me nearly the whole week to pick out the seven demos from the 8000+ I’m really keen for you to play too, but it’s been worth it. I’ve dug through and found some real treasures. The games below are all stunning in completely different ways, and their demos are featured in Steam’s Next Fest until Monday. Also, a whole bunch of them are coming out in the next couple of months! Remember to wishlist anything you like the look of – it’s a huge help for the developers, and for you.

Derelikt

Visuwyg

This is my stand-out game from this latest Next Fest. Derelikt (I wish I were a better person and didn’t just think “I guess you can Derelikt my balls” every time I see the name) is a retro-styled immersive sim, presented as if someone made System Shock for the PS1.

The huge demo begins as you arrive on a space station orbiting Saturn, sent to discover what’s gone wrong, and find a place that’s still occupied by other humans (what a nice surprise!) but also in terrible danger. After a hefty period of exploration, snooping, reading other people’s email, and getting to grips with the mystery, you’re sent into the fray with a gun and a goal. Combat is chunky, ammo isn’t plentiful, and you’re always looking for alternative routes and hidden stashes.

It also has great writing, the best 3D old-school pixel art, and excellent sound. The demo was so long at a certain point I forgot I wasn’t playing the full game, and then was devastated when it finally ended. Thankfully there’s less than a month until its 13th November release.

Kopernicus

Simon Lukasik

I’ve got a bit of a thing for incremental games – those sorts where you do a relatively banal task and then rapidly unlock a massive skill tree of new abilities to speed it up – but rarely recommend them because they’re so thin and transient. Kopernicus is anything but, a deeply interesting and chunky game that applies the incremental theme to a Enter the Gungeon-ish top-down action game, where burning enemies becomes an ever-more interesting prospect as you swell your suite of skills. It’s also stunning, a visceral grotesquery of three-colour art that is deliciously horrific.

This is another demo that is wildly generous, letting you play a huge chunk of the game, and even letting you carry on playing the sizable early stages once you’ve gotten as deep in as it allows. There are so many elements available, building up a base of buildings that open up new upgradeable weapons, tools, and advantages. Upgrades require all manner of collectibles, and some open up new game types (in the demo you have a fuel-gathering game, which itself evolves incrementally the more you play it). It’s so deep and complex and fascinating, and despite its deliberately crude (but brilliant) presentation, a far more involved and challenge game than your typical incremental fare.

The Bagman

Bad Tasty

There’s a lot to be intrigued by here. It’s a traditional-looking point-and-click adventure, where you play as Eloise, a young woman in therapy for some sort of childhood tragedy, but the demo immediately reveals a few twists. You can work with other characters by using a sort of crude, menu-based BASIC-like command structure (“USE metal bar THEN wait for 10 seconds”), then leave them to get on with their task while you do your own. And your task here is to escape a ghoulish animated corpse that’s determined to tear you to pieces.

I’m really intrigued to see how this works as part of a larger story, that includes stealthy elements and action sequences along with inventory puzzles. I’m also intrigued to learn what on Earth anyone involved is thinking by having Eloise look exactly the same as Kathy Rain, complete with blue-to-red hair style. That seems like a weird choice that should probably be addressed before release.

Infested DX

Komi Games

This is an old-school first-person adventure game, presented like your grandpappy used to code, to lovely effect. It’s part verb-picking point-and-clicker, part puzzle game, with (very forgiving) deaths to dodge and inventory items to employ. Set on a space station that’s packed with dangers, gloopy horrors and monstrosities, you solve your way through its VGA challenges while trying not to slice off your fingers in a laser grid.

I like this a lot, and it’s just announced that it’s coming out as soon as 27 October, which just happens to be my birthday!

Hellforged

Moonpyre

This is an incredibly slick bullet heaven meets bullet hell, in which the Vampire Survivors themes trek off in a very Diablo direction. The hub world between levels is a place rather than a menu, and gradually gets filled up with places to improve equipment, people to talk to, and ways to upgrade skills, in a way that’s far more interesting than clicking on icons. But crucially, the core game is just a whole heap of fun, requiring hefty use of your dodge as you simultaneously farm enemies while dodging whole screens full of attacks. Very excited to play this a lot more when there are more arenas in which to battle.

Eldritch Slayer

Mewsturbo

I played a whole bunch of FPS demos, and it’s odd frankly that this is the one that jumped out at me. Eldritch Slayer is such an incredible simple game in most ways, its chunky, cartoon pixel art portraying a dead-straight shooter that’s so lacking in twists that you can’t even look up or down. There’s no jump, no duck, just shooting at the cutest little Eldritch horrors you ever saw and trying not to die.

That not dying part is important here, as each level has no save points, no quick save, just the simple goal of reaching the end in one go. I think that’s what worked for me here, and while the five levels in the demo don’t put up a particular challenge, it’s nice just to go hell for leather without hiding behind saves. It’s all about firing a lot of bullets at a lot of enemies, grabbing health packs when you can, and just ploughing forward in your pursuit of keys, ammo and ladders. I’d love if it had some secrets to find, but other than that, I just really enjoyed its odd purity.

Ruin: 35th Anniversary – Definitive Edition

Official Electric

Some games come with epilepsy warnings, but Ruin feels more like the epileptic fit itself. This extraordinary creation, appearing very briefly int his short demo, is part surrealist adventure game (and I mean “surrealist”, misusing that word is one of my pet peeves), and part art piece. It’s the 35th anniversary edition of a game that never existed before, and that’s by far the least strange thing about this. I loved it.

Final Fantasy Resonance Saves A Great Story From Live-Service Oblivion

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

It's not often that a live-service game gets any sort of attention after the servers have shut down, but there was something special about Final Fantasy: Brave Exvius. It was more than just a mediocre gacha-based RPG with Final Fantasy branding slapped on it--it featured a fully original story with unique characters, a fully fleshed-out setting, and plenty of that unmistakable Final Fantasy flavor. So when Square-Enix announced Final Fantasy Resonance--a full-fledged single-player remake of part of Brave Exvius's story--there was excitement. Former Brave Exvius players could revisit the setting they loved, and those who hadn't played could enjoy the story without all of the gacha baggage.

Retooling a live-service, gacha-monetized game into a single-player experience is no easy task, however, but Square-Enix clearly felt that the legacy of Brave Exvius was worth the effort. And while Final Fantasy Resonance succeeds admirably in retelling the saga of Rain and the battle against the Veritas, it does have a handful of shortcomings that keep it from becoming the next great Final Fantasy epic.

Resonance brings us to the world of Lapis, where adopted brothers Rain and Laswell are doing routine tasks for the Grandshelt Knights. Things quickly escalate, however: The pair encounter a mysterious armor-clad warrior, who's powerful enough to shatter the kingdom's Earth crystal before setting the kingdom's castle ablaze. One near-death encounter and mysterious-young-girl rescue later, Rain and Laswell learn that more armored sentinels are seeking to destroy the remaining crystals that maintain the world to settle a long-simmering grudge. Thus begins a grand adventure with twists, turns, mysteries, revelations, and an ever-growing party of traveling companions--the classic FF formula we've come to love. For the most part, it hews close to the original narrative of Brave Exvius, with some detours and extra exposition along the way. 

In many ways, Final Fantasy Resonance feels engineered as a callback to the olden days of the series. Story- and progression-wise, Resonance feels more in line with the 16-bit Final Fantasy games: mostly linear with some side trekking you can do if you feel so inclined. The narrative pacing is brisk, though it does take time to savor individual character arcs, like Rain and Lasswell’s rivalry, Nichol’s sense of familial duty, and the mysterious circumstances surrounding Rain’s father, Raegen. There are some fun little additions, too, like side quests that offer additional insights into the characters and setting, and on-field conversations you can trigger where characters banter about themselves.Places you visit offer hidden trinkets and treasures if you’re willing to poke around suspicious nooks and take side roads, making exploration satisfying. The HD-2D graphics also feature frequent callbacks to pixel-based Final Fantasy titles that eagle-eyed nostalgia fiends will find delightful. The soundtrack is excellent, offering new versions of classic Final Fantasy songs as well as its own set of defining music leitmotifs. With its presentation, it's easy to imagine Resonance as some sort of lost "middle step" between the pixel and polygon eras of FF.

For the most part, the game is a visual treat: Detailed sprites have fluid animations, and environments are packed with artistic flourishes (the water city Olderion and its surroundings are a personal favorite). The cinematic sequences also attempt to do action and camerawork in ways not yet seen in Square-Enix's HD-2D games. When it works, it looks great, but there are also times when odd-looking compositing of the pixel art over the background or an awkward animation takes you out of the moment. Still, it's hard not to respect the effort, even if there is a lot of reuse of Brave Exvius assets throughout.

So how does Resonance differ from the mobile game it's based on? For starters, the gacha-related element of the game has been completely eliminated. The concept of Visions--characters' spirits who aid the heroes with their unique abilities--still exists, with most of them derived from classic Final Fantasy titles. In Brave Exvius, these Visions were obtained and powered up through gacha — randomized rewards obtained by spending in-game currency (which can also be bought with real money)., but in Resonance you obtain them by visiting shrines hidden throughout the world, then power them up using Magicite items. There's a mix of both familiar faces and original characters in the Visions, though I found it disappointing that only the "main" characters in each FF game (and one beloved villain) got Visions when Brave Exvius was filled with fan favorites.

While Resonance succeeds admirably in retelling the saga of Rain, it does have a handful of shortcomings that keep it from becoming the next great Final Fantasy epic

While Visions were separate units in Brave Exvius, here they act as sort of a modular skillset, able to be equipped and exchanged throughout your party as needed to augment a character's default skills. Every Vision's skill loadout is unique, and by increasing their Mastery Rank through battle, characters can learn and equip individual Vision abilities, even if they aren't using that Vision. For example, when fully mastered, Terra's Vision grants a passive skill that allows characters to break the 9999-damage cap, while Zidane gives his wielder the Steal command. These can be set in a character's ability menu once learned. This system allows for fun character customization, letting you mold characters to your tastes--though one issue is that characters who join the party later have less to work with, simply because they join without any innate Vision skills.

Combat is the other area where Resonance deviates from its Brave Exvius roots. The often chaotic hybrid of turn-based, menu-driven, and tap-to-act combat that the mobile game offered has been completely replaced with a turn-based battle that feels like a hybrid of Persona, Octopath Traveler, and Final Fantasy XIII. 

Enemies have two meters attached to them: a health meter and a stagger meter. Smacking a foe with weaponry or magic depletes health, but it also reduces their stagger bar. The amount of stagger damage you do varies depending on your stats, various buffs, what sort of techniques were used, and so on. Enemies also generally have one (or more) weaknesses, and hitting that weakness deals both increased health damage and significantly boosted stagger damage.

Staggering enemies is important, because not only does it cancel all of their upcoming actions for that round of combat, it also greatly lowers their defense, leaving them open to a world of hurt from your highest-level techniques. The character who triggered the stagger also gets a bonus action at the end of the combat round. If you manage to put all the enemies onfield into stagger, you get the Sweeping Stagger status, which gives your entire party bonus turns, as well as lets you use the ultimate technique of one of the Visions your team has equipped to finish the round. If any enemy managed to survive the round, their stagger meter resets, and the cycle begins again. The benefits of staggering enemies are immense, making it essential to figure out how best to put enemies into stagger (almost always through hitting weaknesses) while keeping your own team safe from their attacks.

It's an interesting combat setup, which is why it's so frustrating when battles become slow-paced slogs. Even sped up, you'll run into fights that take their sweet time, since enemies frequently have high HP--more or less requiring you to stagger them to deal any real damage. (There's no auto-battle option, either.) I understand wanting players to engage with the mechanics, but combined with battles being unavoidable random encounters, it means you're often stuck just menuing through to hit all the weaknesses and hoping it's enough for the Sweeping Stagger so you can summon a Vision to wipe the board clean and move on. 

Compounding this issue is that a decent chunk of encounters simply aren't very interesting. Fights with interesting gimmicks that force you to change up the base strategy of "target stagger bar, heal when needed, then use high-cost high-damage skills during stagger, repeat" are rare, and many of them tend to be optional or late-game encounters. Those encounters are great, and they show the kind of strategic challenge that the combat system is capable of delivering. More often, though, Resonance would rather give you damage-sponge enemies that require little more strategy than patiently poking their weakness over and over again--which means that unknowingly entering certain fights without those skills is a recipe for tedium, since you can't swap Visions or party members mid-fight. (At least you can reload a save from combat.)

But for Resonance's faults, the game otherwise delivered exactly what I wanted--a way for everyone to experience the story, characters, and world of Brave Exvius once again. I can only hope that this is the first step in Square-Enix reviving some of its shuttered mobile games in ways that keep them playable for the future--especially since there's still a lot of story from Brave Exvius that goes beyond the events of this game. As both a remake and a standalone experience, Final Fantasy Resonance shines more than it stumbles.

Dragon’s Dogma 2: Dark Arisen Is Great, But At a Cost

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Capcom choosing the exact same subtitle for two separate expansions in two different games makes a lot of sense. Dragon's Dogma 2: Dark Arisen is not only a thematically relevant title, but thanks to the first game's expansion of the same name, it also conveys to longtime fans what to expect: namely, a new gameplay cycle focused on reworked loot and plenty of dungeon crawling. The key difference this time around is that instead of heading into the labyrinthine depths of a cursed island, you've got an entirely new region to explore.  

As someone who loved Dragon's Dogma 2, I couldn't really ask for much more. Yet changes to some of the game's core systems also dilute what made it so unique when it launched back in 2024. I know it's always been a fairly divisive game, and these changes are intended to make the experience more approachable, but they also strip away some of its magic in an effort to appeal to more people. Dark Arisen is still a great expansion, transporting you to a fascinating new region with more loot to earn, fantastical beasts to slay, and stories to behold, but it also shifts Dragon's Dogma 2 in a slightly different direction. 

To start with, Dark Arisen alleviates some of the game's intentional friction by reducing how often you need to travel on foot. The Eternal Ferrystone was already added to the game a few months ago, letting you freely fast travel to any portcrystal without expending a scarce resource. Those same portcrystals are located at every key location in Dark Arisen's new Norgan region. You still have to reach them on foot first to activate them, but once you do, you can simply teleport from one side of the map to the other. 

Using the Eternal Ferrystone is entirely optional; you can still traverse almost all of Norgan on foot if you desire, so the friction is still there if you want it. However, Dark Arisen also feels designed with unlimited fast travel in mind. The first settlement you come across, for instance, acts as a hub of sorts, letting you manage your gear, rest at the inn, and pick up a fair few side quests, so you're intended to return here often. 

Meanwhile, many quests require you to visit the same locations over and over again to speak with specific characters. Heading off on foot might be an option, but it's never incentivized and feels antithetical to how it wants you to play. 

I'm not a fan of this change. I can acknowledge that giving players more choice is a positive, but the back-and-forth quest design and reliance on fast travel means you never really feel like you have options when traversing Norgan. This has a fairly significant knock-on effect, too: Without embarking on foot more often, I rarely stumbled into the emergent moments that made the base game so thrilling. And without that palpable sense of adventure, Dark Arisen feels less like Dragon's Dogma and more like any number of action-RPGs, rendering this a fundamental change to its identity, rather than an improvement.

When Dragon's Dogma 2 launched, traveling the regions of Vermund and Battahl felt treacherous, especially in the black of night. The world was as hazardous as the enemies you encountered, and campsites provided a welcome respite on your weary travels. Now, those feelings of everpresent danger and welcome safety are mostly gone. There's no risk when you can freely fast travel out of danger or skip a journey entirely. The game's uncompromising design has been softened to broaden its appeal.  

The same goes for changes to its save system. By letting you roll back to a manual "Interim Save" at any time, there's no longer any risk involved in your decision-making. You won't have to live with the consequences of accidentally angering a bunch of guards in Battahl's jail or fret over when to use the Unmaking Arrow. This one-hit-kill item still triggers an autosave as soon as it's fired, creating tension the moment you decide to draw back the bowstring, but the ability to select an earlier save robs it of its finality. If these changes were strictly tied to Dark Arisen, they wouldn't be quite as detrimental. Yet this sanding down of its intentional friction affects the base game, too.

By changing these systems, Capcom has fundamentally altered what made Dragon's Dogma 2 so fantastic. The emergent moments that happened on the road, the interplay between its systems and physics engine, and the need to deal with the consequences of your actions--all of this combined to make the base game an unpredictable and powerful adventure that stood apart from other similar games. I enjoyed its resolute design because it forced me to tackle the world on its own terms, providing a challenge that captured a rousing sense of adventure. I still thoroughly enjoyed Dark Arisen, despite these changes, but there's also this nagging feeling that something's missing.  

Norgan, for its part, has moments where that classic Dragon's Dogma abrasiveness returns. The frigid region offers a nice change of pace from the verdant forests and arid deserts explored previously, with jagged, snow-capped cliffs; frozen rivers; icy tundras; and dilapidated, long-abandoned castles. 

The hyperboreal climate doesn't just provide a visual shift, either; the weather can be both an ally and a foe. Sudden blizzards blanket the screen in heavy snowfall, obscuring your view within a few feet and taking the mini-map out of action. You also can't fast travel during these intense whiteouts, which restores the feeling that you're battling the elements just as much as the ghouls and monsters waiting to ambush you.

On the flip side, you can also use the environment to your advantage, knocking down giant overhead icicles to crush your foes, throwing and skewering them on pointy shards of ice, and rolling boulders downhill that grow larger as they pick up more and more snow. You'll appreciate the extra help when facing down demonic goatmen and abominable snowman-esque monstrosities with gnarly mouths that cover their entire torsos.   

At first glance, Norgan's map might seem fairly small, but there's a sprawling verticality both above and below the surface that hides its true density. New climbing hooks and grapple points have been introduced to help you tackle the elevation changes, while interconnected dungeons and caves reveal shortcuts amid expansive ancient ruins and the piles of loot therein. 

Speaking of which, Dark Arisen entirely reworks the game's loot system with the addition of Relics. You'll unearth this generic gear in chests and on the bodies of defeated enemies, and can then take it to one of the new vendor characters for appraisal, revealing what kind of equipment you've found, its raw stats, and, on occasion, whether it has any special attributes that enhance weapon skills or grant passive abilities. 

This creates a Monster Hunter-esque loop as you explore and defeat powerful foes to gather more Relics, before returning to the appraiser to reveal what you've found and upgrade your character. An unseen dice roll determines the type and quality, and no single piece of gear will remain in use for too long before you'll invariably find something better, but this is still a marked improvement over the base game's uninspired loot system--where I think I purchased more usable weapons and armor than I actually found. 

The base game's map has also received a similar gameplay loop of its own, with the addition of 12 repeatable dungeons known as the Lost Rite Trials. While you can easily travel back and forth between Norgan and Vermund/Battahl, these new dungeons are designed to be tackled before venturing into the snowy north, setting you up with more powerful gear to make the transition easier, with repeat visits netting more and more loot. 

Some of these dungeons are fairly straightforward (reach the end and kill everything along the way), while others rely on different mechanics or require prerequisites before you can enter. One such trial, which asks you to enter as either an Archer or Magick Archer, features rooms filled with support pillars you can shoot down to topple the terrain and wipe out groups of enemies. 

Aside from better gear, completing each Lost Rite Trial nets you an Offering Flame. These can be used at various Obelisks scattered across the frigid Norgan landscape to unlock new vocation skills and stat buffs that boost health, damage, and so on. 

The new skills are welcome, though bumping up the amount of skills you can equip at one time from four to six is a more notable improvement. Having more options at your disposal opens up the game's combat a tad more, and somewhat makes up for the lack of new vocations. As someone who's already maxed out most of the game's classes, though, I realize I might notice the dearth of new ones more than most.

One of the more surprising things about Dark Arisen is how much I enjoyed its self-contained story. Okay, maybe "enjoyed" is the wrong word, but I was definitely more invested in seeing how things would unfold than I was in the base game. It's a solid-enough vehicle for getting you from point A to B, even if it's still just remixing themes from the first game. More characters are involved, and you spend significant time with them, so there's at least something to latch onto. The writing still has little time for subtlety or nuance, but it's appropriately broody, and the story eventually leads you to some fantastic set pieces and locations. 

At over 20 hours long, Dragon's Dogma 2: Dark Arisen is the kind of hefty expansion we don't see very often. I might like this softened version of the game a little less than I did before, but even if some of its prickly nature has been smoothed over, Dark Arisen still offers a whole bunch more of what I loved about Dragon's Dogma 2 in the first place. For first-time players, and those returning with new hopes, it's a more approachable game, and I hope this helps it reach a broader audience. Whichever side of the divide you fall on, there's something here for both. That might sound blasphemous to some, and its vision isn't as unflinching as it was once before, but Dark Arisen--and Dragon's Dogma 2 on the whole--are still well worth your time and attention.

One of the most exciting areas of new, lesser-known gaming right now is in the so-called “incremental” space. These are games that exist in the aftermath of the clicker genre, or “idle games”, a bizarro (and often excellent) world of games about unlocking new skills in order to unlock new skills, all with the ultimate goal of watching numbers go up. As with most genres that strip away many layers of complexity, there will always be a following generation of developers who want to slowly introduce them back, and that’s how you get the incremental game.

Ignoble is such a brilliant example of this. Idle games are known for their enormous skill trees, with squillions of unlocks that dramatically improve your abilities, and developers are now applying this concept to so many other game types. Some of my favourites have included Scritchy Scratchy and it’s daft lottery ticket format, Fortune Mill and its fairground of minigames, and the very cutesy Shelldiver, but none feel as complete and involved as Ignoble, a game about killing absolutely everything, a lot.

It’s an auto-battler, in the sense that there’s no attack button but rather you hit the screens full of enemies by standing close to them. In fact, standing close to enemies is utterly essential, as your health rapidly depletes if you’re ever not immediately next to your foes, which becomes tricky when they’re about to hit you very hard. Each screen has a string of targets to meet, which is to say enemies to kill, which unlocks new events and eventually a boss fight, which when beaten brings you to the next area. And you’ll die an awful lot.

But that’s a good thing, because as you played you also picked up a raft of currencies, including macabre delights like blood, bones, as well as various liquids and jewels, which can then be spent in the skill tree. There are also cards that are rolled for after a run that can be placed in a growing number of slots to add bonuses, and then it’s back in for another go, this time more powerful and better-equipped.

Except, rather brilliantly, the earliest stages of the game don’t stay the same. Each time you defeat a boss, or clear a milestone, the enemies start to change, newer and more powerful ones arrive, and the challenges in a level alter, ensuring you don’t get frustrated by repeating early grinds. At the same time, you get so powerful that the earliest bosses become trivial, so you always feel like you’re making massive progress.

At the point I’ve reached, I’ve unlocked (and in many cases fully levelled up) 105 different skills on the skill tree, and am now double-jumping around with a whirling volley of blades spinning around me, as I cast spells that bring down deathly spikes, while tentacles reach out from my body and slaughter farther enemies… Which is really quite something compared to the trudging guy who slowly mooched about punching enemies when things started.

That sense of constant advancing progress is the compelling key to the incremental genre, and it’s so incredibly effectively executed in Ignoble. This is boosted by fantastic pixel artwork, excellent enemy design and behaviour, wonderful squelchy sound, and an evocative rocky soundtrack. Movement, especially as you unlock all its various aspects, feels slick and fair, and enemies flag their attacks in a neat way.

And there are many other elements I’ve not mentioned, like the way cards are sacrificed, or the excellent way the game changes the shape of the frame throughout, nor the intriguing ways in which bosses are fought. It keeps revealing new aspects to me, and I feel sure it has more surprises to come. And all this for less than a fiver!

I love the way these genres evolve so inventively, and Ignoble is such a brilliant example of how. It so smartly combines the rapid incremental progress with the auto-battling made so popular by Vampire Survivors, and plays like a deeply rewarding roguelite. It’s also just a very satisfying experience in its own right, and splendidly gory with it.
