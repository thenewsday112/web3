James Gunn Says Harley Quinn Will Join the DCU Eventually, and if Margot Robbie Wants to Play Her He'll 'Bend Over Backwards to Explain That in the World'

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

James Gunn has addressed the situation with Margot Robbie’s much-loved take on Harley Quinn, and whether she’s canon in the ongoing DC Universe.

Robbie played Quinn in David Ayer’s 2016 movie, Suicide Squad, 2020’s Birds of Prey, then James Gunn’s sort of sequel, The Suicide Squad. Since then, Gunn has rebooted the DCU, with Quinn so far nowhere to be seen.

The question of whether Robbie’s Quinn is canon is a big one for the DCU, because while some characters from The Suicide Squad have gone on to appear in the DCU (Peacemaker chief among them), not all have. So, is Robbie’s Quinn a part of the DCU or not?

In a special The Suicide Squad: Is It Canon? podcast, Gunn explained the difficulty he has making a call on Robbie’s version of Quinn.

“I think Harley is actually one of the more difficult things in this to justify as being canon in the DCU,” he said.

Quinn is a character who goes hand in hand with Batman’s arch villain, the Joker, who, similarly, has yet to show up in the DCU. The question of whether Quinn and Joker have broken up remains unanswered after some cryptic words from Quinn herself, but, according to Gunn, either way, it would be difficult to introduce Quinn into the DCU without Joker.

“Harley's relationship to the Joker is specific, right?” Gunn said. “Well, we definitely haven't established the Joker yet in the DCU at all. We've established Batman. We've already seen that he exists. We've seen him in Creature Commandos for a glimpse, but we don't know if Joker exists yet. And obviously, Joker is a part of Harley's past.”

But would Margot Robbie even want to reprise her role, should the opportunity present itself? Gunn said “she may be over that,” which doesn’t sound great for Robbie’s role in the DCU.

“Margot Robbie is one of my favorite actors to work with,” he said. “She is so good in the movie. Like, we were watching her last night. We were laughing so hard at so much of the stuff she does. She's just so crazy. I love her as Harley. I don't know if she wants to play Harley again. She may be over that.”

Since playing Quinn, Robbie has gone on to become “kind of the biggest female star in the world,” Gunn continued. “And so, I don't know where Margot's head is with all of this.”

In 2023, Robbie hit even greater heights of fame by playing Barbie in 2023’s box office blockbuster of the same name. More recently, she played Catherine Earnshaw in Wuthering Heights, and is set to star in the Ocean's Eleven prequel.

Still, Gunn would love for Robbie to play Quinn once again, and said he’d make the canon make sense if she fancied giving the character another go.

“Harley is a character I'm almost certainly going to use in the DCU. And if Margot wants to play her, then I'll bend over backwards to explain that in the world,” he said. “But if she doesn't, right, then I might go with somebody else where I don't have to be attached to what happens to Harley in this movie. That's really the situation.

“So what I'm saying is Margot is the one who has to decide whether or not Suicide Squad is canon. This is all on you!”

Elsewhere in the podcast — and this may be related to Harley Quinn — Gunn confirmed that DC Studios is finally getting ready to cast the DCU's Batman.

A girl named Hoshino finds herself back at Another Danchi, a community that’s past its prime. Her grandmother used to run a summer festival for the community, but it’s fallen away, especially with her coming down with dementia. However, the grandmother finds a spark of something familiar in old promotional material that Hoshino finds, and that gives her an idea: maybe she can make her grandmother better by reviving the summer festival. In taking steps, she revives the spirit of the festival Moro-Q, and perhaps together, they can revive Another Danchi’s traditions and revive a grandma’s memories.

Danchi Days is a game developed by sandy powder, Melos Han-Tani, mogumu, and gingham games, and it’s published by Analgesic Productions. I actually played the game for Steam Next Fest last year and I really liked it. Combined with me being a big fan of stuff related to Analgesic Productions, I was excited to hop into this when I was given a code for it.

The structure of Danchi Days has our heroes going around Another Danchi to chat up members of the community to collect RSVPs to turn the summer festival from a little get together to a proper shindig. There’s a chapter structure that expands where Hoshino could go as she slowly builds up an infastructure for the festival, and she comes face to face with potential online haters of the festival. In the meantime, Hoshino finds herself at odds with her childhood friend Rio, who was once much like her but has become openly bitter. But he’s not the only member of the community that’s bitter.

A general mood I read across Danchi Days, even with its cutesy goofiness, is detachment. There are a few friendly faces, sure, but they need to get that little push to get the encouragement to truly engage with the community again. The elderly members of the Another Danchi deals with the pains associated with aging, while a lot of the younger members of the community feel alienated and wants to leave. In particular, Rio had adopted the utilitarian mindset of his father when he got older, shrugging off tradition (a complimentary version of that) and emotion. Danchi Days feels like the story of dying small communities in the face of advancing technology – but perhaps the summer festival will be the jolt of energy that it needs.

And that’s not to say that Danchi Days is all about the need to fully follow tradition. Sometimes tradition is revitalized in a new direction, as Hoshino herself does with full encouragement by Moro-Q. Hoshino invites the aid of an immigrant family and has some real yuri moments with the daughter to provide a new signature food for the festival when the creator of the original food also has dementia and is unable to contribute. A boy with an interest in repairing old plushes takes his interest further by starting a store in Another Danchi’s dying shopping district, while himself giving a jaded old photographer a new lease on life by providing him a new job. Tradition isn’t necessarily bad, but sometimes it needs a burst of something new, no matter how small.

There’s a large variety of characters in Danchi Days to try to connect with, and they’re all weirdos. We have a young woman that loves maintaining the local water fountains. We have a man that insists on morning exercises, even at the expense of his aging body. We have a woman that likes telling lies if the lies indirectly help people. We have a comedian duo that suck at comedy so much that comfort can be found in the silence they leave behind. They provide simple vignettes of living at the danchi, while characters that get involved in the story like that girl Hoshino is in yuri with provides something more substantial. Though, some of the more minor characters do interact if you check up with them, giving a proper feel of a community.

Some of the more one-note characters are actually interesting because you can read reasoning for their interests, like an old man with an obsession with sweeping stuff with a bamboo broom revealing it’s a way to center himself. When confronted with a man paranoid that Something will happen to him, the old man tries to share his interest in the belief that it’d also help him. While there are certainly a few bit characters that the game could have done more with, the overall collection of people is neat.

But you may wonder, how on Earth will you be getting people to RSVP? For some people, it’s a simple conversation. For others, you have to connect with them, and to do that, Hoshino needs to follow her grandma’s belief in feeling things out and finding a mundane common ground to truly connect with others. Hoshino needs to become the ultimate empath.

When you engage with a sensation spouting out stars, Hoshino can feel out the stars to engage with a Sense Minigame. The Sense Minigames are simple minigames themed after what Hoshino’s engaging with, and after clearing the minigame, she walks away with a new understanding of things. The goals are simple, though there is challenge in trying to accomplish the goals by taking as few steps as possible, and it feels like a puzzle game in how you go about doing so. Not using a lot of steps grants stars, and gathering them allows Hoshino passage through gates to engage with mysterious statues of Moro-Q that enables her to empath her way into Moro-Q’s presumable past to see her grandmother’s efforts with him to organize summer festivals – which also showcases its gradual decline.

While that’s one goal to do and there’s challenge in trying to find everyone in town to RSVP with, you can really take your time with things. True to the vibes Hoshino’s chasing, Danchi Day is a cozy game. You don’t have to chase after the Moro-Q statues and you can take your time with everything. With relaxed music and a cutesy style, it’s a very chill – if sometimes depressing – time.

Danchi Day’s status as a cozy game is important to the game’s central thesis: you can find joy in the minor things in life. Hoshino’s grandmother instilled in her the belief that small joys can be found anywhere if you feel it out. The taste of a nice soup. The joy of dancing. The smell of books. The act of finding a four-leaf clover. The relaxing stim of pressing a button. Finishing a sense minigame gives narration about Hoshino’s feelings in engaging with the act, presenting a relatable lens to the players while allowing her to perhaps relate with another person. It’s cozy and comforting, and for these acts to be represented through simple minigames, you can find a simple joy in these acts even if you don’t relate to the feeling Hoshino finds in engaging with these acts.

It’s particularly meaningful considering the disability of Hoshino’s grandmother. As a woman with dementia, she’s forgotten how to do plenty of things. She ran the summer festivals for years, but beyond the growing lack of interest in the community, her disability keeps her from maintaining the tradition. However, despite her dementia, she is still a cheerful person. She still finds joy in the small things, and in reviving the summer festival, Hoshino hopes that the joys of the festival “fixes” her.

What’s interesting though is that the game doesn’t see that as a good thing. Hoshino’s goal is compared to someone outright wishing that her grandmother dies. It seems like an unfair comparison, but the thing is, both lean toward the ableist perspective that disabled people are “broken.” To wish that someone disabled just dies is cold utilitarianism, while wanting to “fix” someone is misguided optimism that still views that disabled person as lesser.

But disabled people are still people regardless of their disability. And in Danchi Days, Hoshino’s grandmother is still a person. She’s left a strong impact on the people around her and even if she can’t actively participate in things anymore, she still lives as a healthy member of society. And in advancing through the game, Hoshino soon realizes that she should respect her grandmother as who she is and value the time they spend together. No matter what happens in life, we just have to roll with the punches and find the mundane joys in life. 

Danchi Days came at an important time for me. I’ve been open about having multiple sclerosis, a neurodegenerative disease like dementia, which has kept me from doing a lot of things. Recently, my mom suddenly died, and it’s something that upended my life. I’m moving to a new place with my sister since my mom was my primary caretaker. My sister’s put a lot on herself to conduct the move and I want to do more to help, but she insisted that I should just do what I can, no matter how mundane my help can be. After all, the small things in life matters.

Playing the game reminded me of the small things in life that I’ve come to treasure. While I don’t have the energy to straight up binge things, I have the free time to enjoy stuff I used to not have the time for (things that I can physically deal with, anyway). I’ve come to truly realize the power of air fryers as somebody scared of burning things down because I can’t stand for too long. I’ve been reading more to keep myself from looking at screens less often and I’m remembering the joys of reading I had back in high school. I’m grateful to have an audience for the things I do because it helps push me to do things when I lack the energy. I’m grateful for my friends and family even though my social batteries burn so easily now, and though I had some sour points with my mom, I’ll forever treasure the good moments I had with her.

Honestly, it’s hard for me to come up with any criticisms of Danchi Days because it came at such a perfect time for me. Maybe Rio could have had more scenes to better develop his perspective with regards to stuff that happens toward the end of the game, but man, I really can’t bring myself to do any nitpicking at all. It’s a feel good game with themes and ideas that resonate so much with me. It’s the first cozy game that I clicked with in a long time, and as someone that’s been pretty open about disliking cozy games, please understand that that means a lot.

If you want something cozy – but not naively wishful – and something simple and sweet, Danchi Days is just perfect.

The Top 10 Best Horror Games To Play This Halloween

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Humans are drawn to horror in all forms, real life and fiction. Perhaps it’s the desire to get as close to death as possible without actually dying; perhaps it’s the morality of life and knowing death comes for all of us; perhaps all our heads are just a little miswired. Whatever the reason, we flock to movies where supernatural entities and puppets on tricycles upend otherwise lovely lives, TV shows where murderous cults kill and stump local detectives, and books where the words on the page seem to follow you to bed. Games, however, go a step further, letting you enter the horror and control it (or become its victim), and you could argue that, as a result, they’re the most immersive form of fiction. 

We at Game Informer love a healthy dose of horror from time to time, and we often review games like this. Some terrify us. Others… don’t. And the best ones stick with us for years and years after the credits. Below is a list of 10 of the best horror games we’ve played recently. This isn’t an all-time list, so don’t fret if your personal favorite isn’t here. Instead, this is a collection of 10 horror games ordered by release date that we think are great for spooky season and beyond. Let us know in the comments what you would add to the list!

Village In The Shade

PlayStation 5, PlayStation 4, Switch, PC

The cozy corner of the horror genre seems to have exploded overnight, and now tons of new slice-of-life horror games are in various stages of development. That said, the team behind newcomer The Village in the Shade is no stranger to the form; they made the Yomawari series, a cute-and-terrifying collection of creepy virtual tales. The Village in the Shade takes things back to the homestead, giving you (a child who emerged from a hole in the ground and was immediately given the responsibility of running an entire farm by yourself) the freedom to plant, sow, reap, ranch, and more. Just follow the important rules: don't go out at night, don't share secrets, and don't leave the village among others. You wouldn't want to break the rules, right? You could, theoretically, go about your idyllic rural living. But as you unravel the threads of what this village is, who you are, and why you're here, it's hard not to get drawn into the strange, mysterious, delightfully disturbing truths hiding in its shadows. Just don't forget to water your turnips before you go sneaking out again.

Tenebris Somnia

PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch, PC

Fans of survival horror and old-school FMV adventure games will find plenty to love (and fear) in Tenebris Somnia. When a young woman visits her ex-boyfriend’s apartment and discovers he's trapped in a hellish dream world, it kicks off a well-executed survival-horror trek of inventive puzzles and resource management to overcome dangerous monsters. High-production, live-action cutscenes help tell the story of a deteriorating relationship and the toll an indie horror film took on its creators, and they play an important role in gameplay. Tenebris Somnia may not look like much due to its classic pixelated presentation, but its scares and story captivate. – Our Review

Resident Evil Requiem

PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch 2, PC

Resident Evil Requiem is not just one of the best Resident Evil games, but it’s also one of the best horror games ever made. It features a unique dual-protagonist structure. Grace Ashcroft is timid, new to the whole “shooting zombies in the head while dealing with Umbrella viruses” thing, and is generally just trying to do her job. Leon S. Kennedy, however, is a veteran of all that and more. Requiem splits its runtime between the two characters, letting you experience both sides of Capcom’s marquee survival-horror style. Grace’s sections leave your hands clammy and your blood pressure high as you desperately try to survive. Leon’s sections leave your hands shaking with adrenaline and your blood pumping with excitement as he mows down zombies (sometimes literally, courtesy of those pesky chainsaws). The result is a Resident Evil game that blends two typical RE experiences – terrifying and tough or exhilarating over-the-top action – into one cohesive package. It’s perfect for someone who wants to feel scared and then soon after receive the catharsis of being the one in control, like being the tragic victim who dies in Act One of the horror movie and the last one standing who lives to tell the tale. – Our Review

Reanimal

PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, Switch 2, PC

Little Nightmares developer Tarsier Studios has a knack for delivering creepy side-scrolling adventures, but Reanimal eschews any whimsy to become its scariest game yet. While mechanically it is similar to Little Nightmares and still puts small children in unimaginable peril, Reanimal is capital-H horror thanks largely to its terrifying threats and disturbing animal-themed imagery (this probably isn’t the game for animal lovers; some messed-up stuff happens to these critters). Escaping the game’s island setting is thrilling and chilling whether you’re playing alone or with a friend in co-op. Months after release, we’re still thinking about Reanimal’s memorable final sequence, which is worth the price of admission on its own. – Our Review

Silent Hill 2 (2024 Remake)

PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC

2001’s Silent Hill 2 is considered one of the best horror video games of all time for good reason. At release, it was a graphical powerhouse, using the PlayStation 2's horsepower to create a setting that begged to be explored, even though every bone in your body said you shouldn’t. The remake from developer Bloober Team took on a big task in modernizing the game without losing the original’s soul, and it did a great job. Among all the recent Silent Hill releases (Silent Hill f, Silent Hill: Townfall), the remake is our favorite. The story of James Sunderland and his troubling relationship with his departed wife is complex, emotional, and – important for the purposes of this list – unsettling and scary. – Our Review

Alan Wake 2

PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC

Alan Wake 2 is an excellent pick for some spooky madness, in the vein of David Lynch’s seminal Twin Peaks mystery-surrealist series. Don’t worry too much about the “2” part of the game’s title. You just need to know that the titular Alan Wake character is a not-great writer who acts like he’s really great, his wife is missing, and he got trapped in the ambiguous Dark Place. He’s only one half of the Alan Wake 2 equation, with his segments set in a dream-like version of New York City. Playing as him feels the most Twin Peaks-y. The other half is Saga, a police officer who arrives in the Pacific Northwest town of Bright Falls to investigate a murder that might be linked to a cult. You can expect more traditional survival-horror gameplay from her. But this is still a Remedy Entertainment game, and without spoiling anything, it goes places. And along the way, you can expect plenty of scares. Turn the lights down low (or off), light a candle, and sit back and enjoy one of Remedy’s best games. – Our Review 

Paranormasight: The Seven Mysteries of Honjo

Switch, PC, iOS, Android

Square Enix has gone two-for-two with its Paranormasight series, an anthology of stories about the strange and occult. And while the more recent Mermaid's Curse is a brilliant tale all its own, the first entry – The Seven Mysteries of Honjo – brings significantly more scares. A group of people in the city of Honjo find themselves caught in a spectral battle royale, using powerful objects and conditions to curse one another, with the allure of a prize at the end. Its magic isn't just in the flowchart timeline, where you can hop between different perspectives and protagonists to see how events play out from every angle, but also in the gorgeous stereoscopic environments and narrative puzzles. Adventure games are a classic genre, and Paranormasight will remind you of what the genre is capable of the moment you slowly spin your camera around, your eyes peeking out from behind your fingers. 

Signalis

PlayStation 4, Xbox One, Switch, PC

Survival horror can contain multitudes, and as the years have worn on, the genre has grown to encompass a wide range of action-combat mechanics. Signalis feels like a re-centering in that respect. It's an isometric survival-horror indie project from developer rose-engine, filled with clever puzzles, scarce resources, limited inventory slots, and looming, oppressive dread. As the Replika unit Elster, you delve deep into a remote complex filled with horrors and monsters, searching for something, or someone, waiting down below. While love for classic horror games and other hallmarks of genre fiction will certainly draw you in, the stylish presentation and devastatingly human core will keep you forging ahead to the finale. Once you've breached the surface of Signalis' retro-future super-structures, you won't be able to turn back.

Immortality

PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC, iOS, Android

In horror movies, audiences often groan at a protagonist for putting themselves in seemingly grave danger just to learn more about a cursed amulet or bloodthirsty killer. But in Immortality, you are the protagonist, and despite a lingering sense that you shouldn't be digging into the archives of these abandoned films, each eerie new scene makes you want to dive deeper. It's a game about Marisa Marcel, an actress who almost starred in three movies in the late 20th century, but has since disappeared. None of the movies were ever released due to mysterious circumstances, but you have access to the archived, unedited live-action footage, including behind-the-scenes conversations. By scrubbing through footage, you can select people or items in each scene and jump to random footage from any other movie that includes the same person or item. The approach to puzzle-solving is unique and keeps you appropriately disoriented. The mystery at the center is an unsettling treat to unravel, and scary in new and unexpected ways. Immortality is one of a kind and an easy recommendation for horror fans who want to try something a little different.

Dead By Daylight

PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PlayStation 4, Xbox One, Switch, PC, iOS, Android

In the decade since launch, Dead by Daylight has continued to succeed, thanks to a dedicated (and newcomer-friendly) fanbase and its towering roster of original and licensed personas from across pop culture horror. The asymmetrical multiplayer title has become an anthology of horror’s biggest faces, including Freddy Krueger, Ghostface, Pyramid Head, and even the Xenomorph. Whether you play as a Survivor, trying to escape all manner of nightmare locations through stealth and cunning, or as a Killer, whose experience feels more like a hunting club, you’ll understand why Dead by Daylight has become a recurring destination for fans of the gruesome and bizarre.

EA Reportedly Closed BioWare To Invest In Developing Star Wars Battlefront III Instead Of The Next Mass Effect

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Electronic Arts shut down the legendary RPG studio BioWare last week, ending development on an untitled fifth Mass Effect game. Since then, we've seen what appears to be a leaked trailer for the game, which suggests it might have been called Mass Effect Universe, and heard a sneak peek at what Grammy-winning composer Austin Wintory was cooking up for the beloved sci-fi RPG series. Over the past week, fans across the internet have tried to piece together how a publisher could decide to shut down a studio as prolific as BioWare, but according to a new Bloomberg report, the answer is Star Wars. 

More specifically, Bloomberg writes that, presented with the opportunity to develop Star Wars Battlefront III with Disney, the publisher, which went private last year after a $55 billion acquisition,  chose to invest its money into that instead of BioWare's in-development Mass Effect sequel. The publication writes that EA looked at its portfolio for the coming years and had two massive sci-fi investments it could continue: Star Wars Battlefront III or BioWare's next Mass Effect. Even though Mass Effect was well into development, and even though the Battlefront threequel hasn't even entered development, EA chose to continue with the latter. And so BioWare was shut down, and Mass Effect development ended, though Bloomberg's sources say there are other, less significant reasons the studio was shuttered, too. 

Those other factors include the poor reception to 2017's Mass Effect: Andromeda, the failure of Anthem in 2019, and 2024's Dragon Age: The Veilguard, which, despite garnering positive critical reception, apparently missed EA's sales expectations by 50%. 

There's also the uncomfortable reality that BioWare made progressive LGBTQIA+ positive games but was a studio within a publisher house now partially owned by the Public Investment Fund of Saudi Arabia, a government fund from a country where same-sex activity, including marriage, is criminalized, sometimes punishable by death; these harsh laws extend to other facets of the LGBTQIA+ community. Another investment partner in this acquisition of EA is Affinity Partners, a Miami-based investment firm founded and run by former White House senior advisor Jared Kushner, who is the son-in-law of President Donald Trump, a political figure who imposes laws that discriminate against the LGBTQIA+ community, especially the 1% of Americans who are transgender; Trump has also been known to mock this community in various ways. No official statements say BioWare shut down because of its progressive leanings, but one has to wonder if that was a factor, especially after reports that BioWare scrubbed diversity from demos to avoid negative feedback from EA (thanks, Kotaku).

When BioWare closed last week, only 50 employees remained, all working on the next Mass Effect, after years of layoffs at the studio.

Nocturne Episodes 1 & 2 Recap and Review

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Spoilers follow for Nocturne Episodes 1 and 2, which are available on Apple TV now.

Apple TV’s new thriller series, Nocturne, aims to draw audiences in with just the right balance of the familiar and the unexpected. The show asks what happens when a boy thought to be the victim of a serial killer returns alive, and follows the criminal mastermind as he maneuvers law enforcement during their race to save any other victims who may still be out there. Over the course of these first two episodes, viewers are left with questions — a mix of plot-driven intrigue, interest in character development, and a touch of side-eye around potential logic gaps or plot conveniences.

Nocturne is based on Lars Kepler’s crime novels (The Sandman and Lazarus), and adapted by Rowan Joffe and John Hlavin for TV. These debut episodes lay the groundwork for convicted killer Jurek Walter’s unfolding scheme to free himself (he is astonishingly well played by Stephen Graham), set against multiple law enforcement officers’ attempts to a) stop him from doing more harm and b) find any of the still-living victims. Jurek was convicted of a series of brutal killings, which devastated a small town. He managed to get a jury to buy into an insanity defense, or we must presume he did, as he’s in a psychiatric hospital rather than a prison. (Side note: how he pulled that off is a nagging question, as nothing in his demeanor suggests an insanity defense would fly… If it’s simply that he’s a sociopath, wouldn’t that apply to all killers? Moving on.)

From a technical perspective, the show does a beautiful job of dropping us in this world. The opening shot of Episode 1, “Lazarus, Come Forth,” establishes a sense of foreboding, danger, and claustrophobia that will become a refrain throughout the series. We get the sense that the creature we are encountering is not unlike a lion in a cage. The camera moves down a cold, concrete hallway toward cells that feel (purposefully) hidden and forgotten — the shot is all the more suffocating because the dark walls of the cell entryways initially obscure the sides of the frame. This is our introduction to Jurek, and it’s notable that the series starts by putting us in the mindset of the killer. He feels trapped and eager to get out. For some yet-to-be-revealed purpose, his gritty, simultaneously menacing and soothing voice whispers to the patient next door, luring the man to seek freedom by any means necessary, even with violence (in this case, against himself). This is Jurek’s central arc across the first two episodes.

He is brilliant and adept at psychological warfare. He also seems to believe he’s entitled to his freedom, or at the very least that there is some legal loophole that will get his conviction overturned if only he can expose the lie that former homicide detective Jonah Lynn (Liev Schreiber) told to seal Jurek’s fate. Jurek taunts Jonah towards the close of the first episode with an offer of “the truth for the truth” when Jonah and Special Agent Quinn (Poorna Jagannathan, lately of Lanterns) visit him. In other words, Jurek will help them find more of his victims, specifically Felicia Frost — the missing sister of Michael, the boy who’s come back from the dead — if Jonah confesses to lying in court. This raises the stakes for everyone, including Agent Quinn, who’s already taken a risk by bringing a retired cop she doesn’t know this close to her case. If his old lie blows it up, that’s on her, with a missing girl’s life on the line.

While some crime dramas give us only as much information as the characters searching for answers have, this one puts the audience a step ahead. It’s an interesting choice, and may ultimately be a misdirect.

While some crime dramas give the audience only as much information as the characters searching for answers have, this one puts the audience a step ahead. It’s an interesting choice, and may ultimately be a misdirect. Here’s what appears to have happened: a hospital worker named Karl (Rory Culkin) is assigned to the team that oversees Jurek. Their first encounter, like others in the series, is crafted with impressive tension: Karl searches for a missing weapon beneath Jurek’s metal bed, gets caught by his pant leg, and can’t free himself before Jurek comes to from a sedative — the trapped animal motif plays throughout. Before the scene is over, Jurek has pressed a note into Karl’s pocket, urging him to smuggle it out to his lawyer (more on the questions this raises in a bit).

The note Jurek hands Karl seems to set everything in motion: the strong implication is that, lawyer or no lawyer, it contained Jurek’s instruction to an accomplice to free Michael. Jonah is actively drawn back into the case when Michael turns up alive, as the children’s mother, Ida Frost (Chrissy Metz), and the residents of the town trust him more than anyone else in law enforcement. Also advocating for his participation is FBI agent Saga Bauer (Zazie Beetz), his goddaughter, the child of his deceased partner, and someone he effectively raised. (She eventually goes undercover as a patient in Jurek’s secured ward in order to ascertain who is leaking messages and endangering lives.)

The reality is, Jonah has clearly never given up on finding the remaining missing persons associated with the case: as Agent Bauer discovers via his giant murder board, he’s been methodically searching an enormous grid, single-handedly, for years. The case is deeply personal for both of them. Jonah carries guilt for having quit the force after the (seemingly unrelated?) accidental death of his wife and child. And Jurek (or rather his accomplice) was responsible for kidnapping Bauer’s stepmother, Rebecca. It’s also implied that Bauer’s father committed suicide after his wife’s disappearance. Through Agent Quinn’s vocal skepticism, the show addresses the inappropriateness of having either Jonah or Bauer involved in the investigation, given their personal connections to Jurek. The story excuses the conflict of interest (not to mention that Jonah is no longer an officer of the law) by pointing to their familiarity with the case and the ticking clock on saving Felicia. The dynamics and interwoven tragedies all tied to Jurek stretch credulity, but the production value and strength of the performances are enough to keep the viewer engaged through a good deal of character, plot, and relationship table-setting. At its core, this is a battle of three savants with real stakes: two on the victims’ side, deploying their individual skills to best a mysterious, murderous genius. That brings us to the tightrope the series walks.

When crime dramas lean on preternatural intellect, whether it’s Sherlock Holmes or his foil, Moriarty, they run the risk of reading as a lazy sort of magic trick, where the detective or killer effortlessly “knows things” they’d need to in order to keep the story moving. Indeed, Jonah’s near-instant recognition of where Michael was being held, and Jurek’s Derren Brown-level ability to read people (pegging Karl’s guitar playing and Jonah’s partner’s violent alcoholism), open the door to the kind of conveniences that could lose a modern audience as the series unfolds. It already causes one to wonder: if Jurek is in possession of that kind of mental acuity, why did he get caught while his partner went free? Fortunately, Graham, who stunned the world with Adolescence last year, is deeply compelling, allowing us to give ourselves over to his performance.

That isn’t to undercut the potent talent of the rest of the cast, or the visual storytelling. The show is beautifully shot, with well-crafted production design. It immerses us in a rustic exterior world that vacillates between gray and worn, and comfortingly familiar. The forest landscapes are striking, but dense and dangerous in a way that mirrors the cold, stifling confines of the hospital Jurek is housed in.

Schreiber is delivering a grounded, understated portrayal of a grieving man who is just a bit too tired to fully fight against terrible ideas (Bauer’s undercover operation) and far too invested to stop himself from digging, with or without permission. One moment that stands out is when Agent Quinn tells Jonah she’s sorry for the loss of his wife and child; he nods, and unbearable pain flashes in his eyes for an instant before he moves on. Beetz is convincing and deeply affecting as a chameleon-like agent willing to harm herself (physically or otherwise) to close a case. It’s clear that this particular operation may drive her to a breaking point for multiple reasons: Jurek’s uncanny ability to read others and play on their weaknesses, her own stepmother’s disappearance (which he is connected to), and, not least of all, that it taps into the trauma of a woman who was the child of a mentally ill mother and addict father. That fear comes through as she prepares to enter the ward, right after visiting her mother. Beetz gives the sense that Bauer is unsure whether she’s crossing a line she won’t be able to come back from, where fact and fiction are too close. Bill Camp is also endearing as Chief Ellison, particularly as he talks about what the Frost kids meant to the town; he looks vulnerable, like a man who knows he is out over his skis, but wishes he wasn’t.

It took a moment for me to recognize Culkin as the compassionate nurse, Karl. Physically, his hair is doing a great deal to support the transformation, but this is also a side of the actor less seen on screen. There was one moment, when he first sees Jurek on the security cameras, in which I was left to wonder if he had some nefarious intent. But for the most part, he reads as a man of incredible kindness and skill with patients whom most of society writes off and exiles. That said, it’s odd that an employee who demonstrates an extraordinary amount of empathy is tapped for a job that puts him right in the crosshairs of a person known to exploit those instincts, and in a dangerous environment that requires a certain degree of toughness. Additionally, Karl, who is clearly driven by financial need (he accepts the new job the moment he’s informed that it comes with a raise and tells Jurek he can’t afford to lose it), inexplicably risks his career for a dangerous criminal he just met.

So, that leaves us with a couple of options: this is a piece of plotting that doesn’t logically track, or Karl isn’t who we think he is. If it’s the latter, perhaps Karl was “sent” into the hospital with purpose. It’s strangely convenient that Jurek’s day nurse went missing (and is likely dead), making room for Karl to take over. Theoretically, the patient who injures himself in the opening sequence could have gotten word to Jurek’s partner to get rid of the day nurse and make room for Karl. But how could Jurek have known that the exact right personality type for his purposes was working a few floors above him, let alone that he’d get the job? Was Karl a plant, then, and Jurek’s accomplice? That doesn’t square with their first encounter, where, with no witnesses, Jurek treats Karl as a stranger and insists he is still a human deserving care. And if someone else on staff was steering Karl, Jurek already has a partner on the inside, so why would he need Karl? Those are just a few of the questions that may or may not pay off at the close of the series. There are smaller dangling threads as well. For example, Jonah finds footprints both in the building where Michael was held and outside the missing day nurse’s home, so why in the world would he hold them back from the FBI? Ostensibly, he’d do anything to find Felicia, so that type of behavior simply doesn’t track. Ironically, the serial killer is doing a better job of asking for help and relying on a partner.

That said, the series works in no small part because Graham is simply impossible to look away from. He’s playing a killer who appears to have a (forgive the Dexter reference) code that makes sense to him. He says he “wants people to pay for their crimes.” Yes, that was in reference to Jonah’s “lie,” but the way he looks at the nurse who is rude to Karl reminds me of Hannibal Lecter’s deep disdain for bad manners. Graham’s depiction of Jurek is utterly magnetic and mercurial; when Jonah and Quinn visit, he breaks into a fit of giggles at the idea that she’s a “special” agent, as if the word itself is a joke, and moments later he’s commanding the room, telling them with understated menace that they’re only there because he brought them there. He really does seem to own them. The minute he’s off the screen, I crave more time with him.

This entirely serves the series, because it allows us to believe that others could fall under his spell, even Bauer, a smart, capable, stony FBI agent with oceans to lose. There is a deep irony that he’s maneuvering and clawing his way to freedom, given his brutal kill method of burying victims alive. He complains that he’s been in solitary, but his partner has kept a child in the dark so long that the boy went temporarily blind. There’s also a very small possibility that he is, in some way, innocent, and that he has been “made worse” by his circumstances. Either way, for those on both sides of the law, this is a story about freedom, loss, redemption, and revenge, and the first two episodes have given me enough to stick with it for now.

Charting the Unknown also deepens life in the world. Kliff, Oongka, and Damiane can form deeper bonds with some of Pywel’s inhabitants. Interact with them to build trust and strengthen your relationship, which may eventually blossom into romance and even marriage.

An upgraded housing system now lets you build your house from the ground up. And from the layout of the interior to the placement of walls, windows, and other structural elements, you have more control than ever to design your home, sweet home. There’s more: select castles, once liberated, can become yours. This will allow you to decorate their courtyards and interiors with unique objects and furnishings, turning a hard-won stronghold into another place you can call your own.

Also, if you want to take a more active role in the strongholds you have liberated, you can lease certain facilities such as a restaurant or a smithy and run them yourself. Assign equipment and comrades, decide which goods to sell, and grow each location’s recognition.

Garden of Trials

For those who don’t always like life too peaceful, Charting the Unknown has new content for you. The Garden of Trials is a combat-focused challenge where you face the bosses you have met throughout your adventures in Pywel, one after another. So test your skills and mettle to see if you hold up against these formidable foes.

Whether you are a seasoned Greymane or just taking your first steps in Pywel, there is plenty to discover.
