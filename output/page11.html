According to a report from The Hollywood Reporter, actor Sir Patrick Stewart has passed away at the age of 86. Stewart spokesperson, Kelly Bush Novak, shared that he died after “a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia.”

Stewart was best known for his roles as Jean-Luc Picard in Star Trek: The Next Generation (and its various spinoffs) and Charles Xavier in the X-Men films, but he did plenty of video game performing, too. Stewart played Emperor Uriel Septim VII in The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion, one of the first characters you meet in the game, and Zobek in Castlevania: Lords of Shadow. He also played Jean-Luc Picard in about 10 different Star Trek games and Charles Xavier in five different X-Men games.

Patrick Stewart as Jean-Luc Picard in the 1994 Super Nintendo game, Star Trek: The Next Generation.

Stewart will always be a crucial voice and face in the legacy of sci-fi entertainment. Jean-Luc Picard will undoubtedly be an enduring and important character in the Star Trek franchise, and that's thanks to Stewart's portrayal of the starship captain. His portrayal of Charles Xavier also helped to legitimize the comic book movie, and he will appear again as the character in this year's upcoming Avengers: Doomsday.

Ubisoft has released a fall update on the Assassin’s Creed franchise, providing news big and small on its flagship series. The news includes updates for existing games and smaller but significant insights on the various upcoming Assassin’s Creed games previously announced.

Assassin's Creed Valhalla

In terms of current games, Assassin’s Creed Valhalla is getting a long-requested New Game Plus thanks to a new update coming on November 5.   • An optional progression mode where Gear, Runes, and Masteries all contribute to Eivor's overall Power
  • New runes, achievements, a stash system, and new rewards, including a Master Assassin outfit inspired by Naoe's look in Assassin's Creed Shadows

This news comes a few days after New Game Plus arrived in Assassin's Creed Black Flag Resynced. 

Assassin's Creed Codename Invictis

Codname Invictis, a PvP multiplayer game, remains in development as Ubisoft shares it has incorporated feedback from players who, according to Ubisoft, “saw great potential in the foundations but wanted more of the pillars that define Assassin's Creed.” To this end, Ubisoft has spent months reworking Invictis to reflect this vague feedback and say the game is “now evolving in an exciting direction that better reflects those pillars while staying true to its multiplayer ambitions.”

Assassin's Creed Codename Hexe

As for the next single-player Assassin’s Creed, Codename Hexe, Ubisoft says it will share the next major update on this game in 2027. First announced in 2022, Codename Hexe is set during the 17th-century reign of the Holy Roman Empire, and the era's witch hunts will play a significant role.

Future Assassin's Creed Remakes 

With the release of Assassin’s Creed Black Flag Resynced earlier this year, Ubisoft has given fans what they'll likely see as a disappointing update on potential future remakes: “One thing we want to be transparent about today is that we currently have no other Assassin's Creed remake actively in development. However, Ubisoft adds that this door isn’t closed for good. “Like many of you, we love the idea of revisiting our classic titles, and we'll keep exploring opportunities to bring older titles back,” says Ubisoft.

Assassin's Creed Netflix Series

Ubisoft’s next update concerns the live-action Assassin's Creed series in production for Netflix. They say the show is “nearing completion” in Rome and that award-winning composer Hannah Peel (Insomnia, Rogue Agent, Game of Thrones: The Last Watch) is composing the score. 

Lastly, Ubisoft teases big celebrations are planned for 2027, which marks the 20th anniversary of the Assassin's Creed franchise. It's keeping details to itself for now, but states it's "preparing a year of reveals, updates, rewards, and community moments. "

This season of South Park America started off well enough, but there's been a real struggle to build any real momentum over the long haul. It often feels like the writers aren't sure whether to fully commit to the idea of doing more classical standalone episodes this year or try to build an overarching narrative. And so we're left with a season that constantly reminds us that "November is coming," without anything more insightful to add than the constant allusions to Grand Theft Auto 6.

To its credit, Episode 4 tries to build on the foundation of the previous chapters and establish a more coherent conflict for the embattled South America. This time around, rising beef prices and high demand have robbed South America Elementary of its precious sloppy joes. It falls on Stan, Kyle, Cartman, and yes, Little Randy, to save the day and find a solution to the town's woes.

The results of these efforts are mixed, at best. The South American beef storyline relies a little too much on hitting the same gags over and over again. Seeing rich snobs devour South American beef and promptly explode in a shower of buttery diarrhea is funny the first couple of times, but it gets old pretty quickly without any real escalation. The same goes for the constant "South American beef" wordplay. I will say, though, that I got a big kick out of the heated steakhouse rivalry and the conflict between Ruth's Chris, Sizzler, and Texas Roadhouse. The same goes for the prolonged dig at rich, suburban wannabe cowboys.

Again, I just wish all of this built to something a little more memorable, coherent, and politically insightful. Maybe it would have been better if the series waited a little longer before coming back.

What South Park does have right now, though, is Little Randy. I was hoping that particular twist from Episode 3 would carry over, and boy does it. It's a lot of fun seeing Randy embrace his new role as a beer-chugging fourth grader, and in watching Stan deal with the constant embarrassment of having his father hanging around. Amusingly enough, he's the only one who seems to actually resent Little Randy's presence.

I especially enjoyed Randy's attempts to cement his new status in the friend group, crowning them "Goonies" and referring to Cartman as "Chunk." Cartman's line, "Why does your dad keep talking about gooning?" is easily the highlight of the entire episode. As much as the series may be struggling on the political and topical humor front right now, there's a lot of potential to mine with Randy's latest little crusade. Hopefully these next two episodes can take full advantage of that potential.

The iPhone 18 Pro Max Takes an Incredible Design and Just Makes It Faster

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every year there’s a new iPhone, and while the iPhone Duo is a huge departure from what Apple has done in the past, the iPhone 18 Pro Max is nearly identical to last year’s model. Apple didn’t do much to drastically change the Pro Max, but instead tweaked some things under the phone to make it a bit more useful.

The most obvious improvement is the A20 Pro, which is significantly stronger than the A19 Pro in last year’s phone. But the most useful improvement has been to Siri. The improvements Apple has made to its ‘Apple Intelligence’ has made Siri more useful than it has been since the company first tried revamping the assistant with AI back in 2024.

So, if you’ve already upgraded to the iPhone 17 Pro last year, there isn’t much reason to upgrade again. But if you’ve been on the fence and need an upgrade, you could really do a lot worse than the iPhone 18 Pro Max.

Design and Features

When I say that the iPhone 18 Pro Max looks just like the iPhone 17 Pro Max, I mean it. Even if I put the two phones on a table next to each other, you’d be hard-pressed to figure out which is which. They both measure 0.34 inches thick, even though the 18 Pro Max is a bit heavier, at 8.78 ounces, compared to the 17's 8.22 ounces. The only way I can tell my iPhone 17 Pro Max from the iPhone 18 Pro Max is that the Pro Max Apple sent me for review has the new Black colorway.

But while the phone’s chassis being unchanged might sound like a bad thing, it really is a case of not fixing something that’s not broken. The best part of the iPhone 17 Pro Max was its camera shelf, which virtually eliminates the phone wobbling when it’s put down on a table. For some reason, other phone manufacturers really haven’t adopted this design language yet, but I’ll just say every time I use an Android device lately, I miss the camera shelf, however absurd that may sound.

The rest of the iPhone 18 Pro Max’s layout is also unchanged. On the right side of the phone, you still get the power/lock button, along with the capacitive Camera Control Button. And just like on the 17 Pro Max, the camera button is extremely easy to hit on accident, so be mindful of it.

Over on the left side is the volume rocker and the Action Button, which I have set to enable or disable Do Not Disturb mode. You can go into the settings and swap it to one of 12 different actions, or just disable it entirely. But I like to keep it on DnD, so that I can make my phone stop blowing up when I’m overwhelmed.

The iPhone 18 Pro Max’s display is also unchanged, at 2868 x 1320, and with a gorgeous OLED panel. Everything I watch or play on the phone looks incredible, with absolutely vibrant colors. It’s also rated for up to 3,000 nits of brightness, which means it gets bright enough to use outside comfortably. The only other phone I’ve used that comes close is the Samsung Galaxy S26 Ultra, but that shouldn’t be too surprising, given that Samsung manufactures a lot of Apple’s displays.

Apple has also shrunk the Dynamic Island on the display, so you have less of the display area blacked out at the top of the display. It is still there, and it’s still visible, but I don’t really notice the difference until I have the 18 Pro and the 17 Pro next to each other. There have been phones that have eliminated the need for a camera cutout, and the iPhone still isn’t there, but hopefully by shrinking it, Apple is getting closer to getting rid of it altogether.

Siri Is Better

I have had some problems with Siri for a few years now. For some reason, around 2022 or 2023, Apple's voice assistant stopped working for me most of the time I tried to use it. And it's not like I'm constantly trying to talk to the little robot in my phone or anything, but it got so bad that I couldn't even yell at my phone to skip a song on Apple Music reliably when I was in the shower.

But with the improvements to Apple Intelligence in iOS 27, Siri has dramatically improved. I am no longer asking it to set a timer, only to have it hang and then ask me if I want to query ChatGPT. It just sets the damn timer. I don't want to give Apple too many roses for this, because in a lot of ways, Siri has simply regained functionality that it had a decade ago.

Of course, Apple has also worked in a bunch of AI features, as well, which will let Siri scan through your messages or photos to help you plan events or find information. And, I'm sure that's useful for a lot of people, and it does appear to work well, but I'm old and too used to doing everything manually, and even this new version of 'Apple Intelligence' hasn't converted me to automating everything I do on my phone.

Camera

For the most part, the camera system in the iPhone 18 Pro Max is exactly the same as the iPhone 17 Pro Max, with the only real difference being the addition of a variable aperture. The aperture on the 18 Pro works just like it does on a regular camera, with a mechanical shutter that widens or narrows to control the amount of light in the shot.

For most people, this probably won’t make much of a difference, and the auto aperture setting is going to work fine. However, if you like to have more control of your photos, Apple has added ‘Pro controls’ to the camera app, that lets you dial in the aperture for your shot. It works extremely well, but I still don’t think I’m going to abandon my DSLR and rely on the iPhone cameras. Though, it should come in handy at events like CES, where I typically need to take photos on my phone in the interest of time.

The camera itself is still great, though, and photos look great without really needing to spend a lot of time finding the perfect shot. It’s just nice that Apple has added some finer control for anyone that wants it.

Gaming and Performance

The iPhone 18 Pro Max is powered by the A20 Pro, along with 12GB of RAM, and it is surprising just how much performance Apple was able to squeeze out of it. In every test I’ve run on the new Pro, it far surpasses its predecessor.

In GeekBench 7, the iPhone 18 Pro Max gets a single-core score of 3867 points and a multi-core score of 11,204 points, compared to the 17 Pro, which gets 2,865 and 7,120 points, respectively. That’s a massive 44% improvement to multi-core performance, and a 29% improvement to single-core performance.

Even compared to the Samsung Galaxy S26 Ultra the iPhone 18 Pro Max is 24% faster, with the Android device getting 2,725 points for single-core and 8,786 points for multi-core.

When it comes to gaming, the GPU stapled to the A20 Pro is also impressive, with the 18 Pro scoring 8001 points in the Wild Life Extreme test, compared to 4795 from the 17 Pro Max and 5758 points from the Galaxy S26 Ultra. That makes the 18 Pro Max 33% faster than Samsung’s 2026 flagship.

Just like the 17 Pro, the 18 Pro Max is cooled by a vapor chamber, and it's even slightly larger this year. But, even though it has more surface area, the iPhone 18 Pro isn't more stable than last year's model, coming in at 66% in the 3DMark Steel Nomad Light stress test. That's just a bit worse than the 17 Pro Max, which got 69% in the same test last year, but with how much faster the A20 Pro is, I'll take it.

Apple also included a bigger battery this year, with 5,567mAh of capacity, compared to 5,088mAh in the iPhone 17 Pro Max. Apple claims that this battery will last through 30 hours of regular use, and while I don’t have a reliable way to benchmark battery life, I believe Apple’s numbers.

I have been pushing this phone for around three weeks now, and I usually only put it on the charger every other day, and the phone hasn’t died on me yet. Obviously, if you’re taking a lot of photos or playing a heavy game like Where Winds Meet, that number will go down, but even then I don’t imagine the phone dying in the course of a day.

Purchasing Guide

The iPhone 18 Pro Max is available today, starting at $1,299, which will get you 256GB of storage. You can also get the iPhone 18 Pro, with a smaller display, starting at $1,199.

Phantom Blade Zero: How boss weapons transform combat and exploration

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Phantom Blade Zero blends influences from a variety of sources to create intense, fast-paced combat and a supernaturally infused Kungfupunk world. It draws on the tough, powerful bosses of Soulslikes, the quick, combo-focused combat of hack-and-slash games, the fascinating stories and characters of RPGs, and the unlockable exploration of search action games.

All those ideas come together to create something familiar and unexpected, reworking common action-RPG elements into beautiful, fast-paced battles across a strange, devastated world.

Mixing aggression and defense

While Phantom Blade Zero’s combat evokes games like Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice and Bloodborne, it takes a more hybrid approach, channeling Wuxia stories and kung-fu movies. You’re not just using R1 to dodge, holding L1 to block blows, or tapping L1 to parry them; you also need to press attack, using Square for basic attacks and Triangle for more powerful blows to create simple but effective combos and force your opponent to block and parry as well.

It’s all about managing a meter measuring your Sha-Chi. Blocking attacks depletes your Sha-Chi, and if it runs out, you can be staggered and left open to attack — but perfectly parrying, and dodging and landing blows knocks down your opponent’s Sha-Chi instead, letting you cut through their defenses. 

You can quickly refill your Sha-Chi by pressing Left on the D-Pad to swap between your two equipped main weapons. The system pushes you to string attacks together from both weapons, or to swap as you end your strikes so you’re ready to start parrying.

As you land attacks, you also earn Sha-Chi Essence. When it’s fully charged, you can unleash a Power Surge attack with L2 that overpowers your opponent, interrupts their attacks, and deals massive damage. All those elements, combined with powerful Phantom Edge weapons that have specific but limited uses, work together to turn battles into a fluid dance that constantly moves between attack and defense. And thanks to haptic feedback in the DualSense controller, you feel those clashing blades, adding intensity that shows how powerful and lethal their blows can be.

Using the weapons of the enemy

One of the best parts of Phantom Blade Zero is the way it dishes out new weapons. Instead of finding swords in treasure chests or buying them from merchants, you claim most of your weapons from bosses you defeat. You get to see exactly how a weapon works as you defend against its unique capabilities, then try those capabilities yourself.

For instance, after you defeat the boss Hua, Deadly Betrothed, you can claim her Scarlet Wraithblade, which summons spider lilies on the ground as you complete combos that slow down enemies and let you control the battlefield.

The White Serpent and Crimson Viper are dual-wielded swords, and you can use each one’s separate combo, or mix them together for different effects that let you use one sword up close and send the other spinning in circles around you.

Each weapon offers its own fighting style, special combos, and status effects, like causing enemies to bleed for extra damage or allowing you to siphon their life. With so many varied combat options, you’ll constantly want to try different weapons and see how they exploit the weaknesses of the tough bosses you’ll face.

Uncovering the mysteries of the Wulin

Exploration is also a big part of the experience. The game is full of side quests and stories to chase down that flesh out the Wulin, the game’s world, and the characters you meet along the way.

Each area you visit is expansive, with lots of side paths and secrets to find. Often, you’ll come across areas you can’t reach yet, encouraging you to backtrack later after finding an essential item. Some paths are blocked by specific Phantom Edge weapons that you’ll earn from defeating bosses, making it possible to smash through a barrier or grapple up to a distant location.

Phantom Blade Zero ties its storytelling, exploration, and combat together by rewarding you for your curiosity. Exploring new areas will often advance old side quests and uncover new ones. Discovering Blue Memory Flowers scattered throughout the world gives you glimpses into the past that unlock the stories of the places you visit. Finding all the flowers for a given story also increases Soul’s health, making him more powerful for the next battle.

A dark and tragic world

The Kungfupunk aesthetic informs not only how Phantom Blade Zero looks, but also the stories you find within it — it’s a dark place, constantly steeped in tragedy. Those stories lead to epic, varied boss fights against powerful martial artists and corrupted, monstrous characters.

Hua, Deadly Betrothed, is an early optional boss who forces you to stay aware of your surroundings as she summons deadly spider lilies that sap your strength and stagger you, leaving you open to massive blows from her huge sword. Once you defeat her, you learn about how she was used by the man she was to marry, and her rage resulted in the massacre of the entire wedding party.

Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 DMZ Review

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This is a standalone review that covers only Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4's DMZ mode. You can find our campaign and multiplayer review here.

Extraction shooters can feel a little like a modern trend, with some heavy hitters like Marathon and Arc Raiders shaking the table within the last year or so, but they’ve been a growing experiment in the shooter space for the past decade or so. Call of Duty dabbled in it with the original DMZ back in 2022 (you could even argue they were late to the party by then), and its return in 2026 is timely. I’ve logged quite a few bootsteps through the bombed out streets, scattered woodlands, and high security bases of this big new mode over the last week, and after hours of vehicle hijacking, SAM site hacking, and target elimination, I can say this bigger, better DMZ is a welcome newcomer to the extraction shooter slate.

The PvPvE dynamic of extraction shooters often lives and dies by how well the mission and level design encourages you to explore, rewards you for overcoming challenges, and guides you to interact with others in order to keep those spoils. The fictional Korean Hajin region your exploits take place in is well suited for this. Its dense pockets of urban development – a village in the north, the dangerous main city in the east, an isolated prison out west – are separated by rocky forests concealing all sorts of militarized checkpoints and other points of interest hiding loot to shove into your pockets.

Parts of it look a little samey, the consequence of modern urban development where (at least from the outside) most buildings look the same, especially compared to some of DMZ’s big sci-fi competitors that go above and beyond to look striking at first glance. But the map is so big that you could spend the entirety of your approximately half-hour runs in only a fraction of it. And where Battle Royales (like CoD’s own Warzone) move players around by having an encroaching death cloud forcing everyone into a smaller and smaller area, DMZ instead entices squads to adventure with its Dynamic Operations.

Dynamic Ops are spontaneous missions that appear as you travel across the battlefield which come in lots of forms, from securing a package to blowing up a target to escorting a VIP. Many of them are multi-stage affairs that have your fireteam moving from point to point as NPC enemies ramp up a more coordinated defense against you. These all get rewarded by supply drops that grant you loot, the value of which escalates based on how many of them you successfully complete. I prefer this sort of objective chasing to the more standard task lists in other games like this (which also exist here, in fairness) because it makes for more engaging individual runs. I’ll prepare the best I can beforehand, but can’t be ready for every possible string of operations once I deploy.

There are many actions and events, like aforementioned supply drops, that are broadcast to everyone within earshot, hypothetically to tempt other players to be nosy (and potentially belligerent). Despite those lures, open skirmishes with others have been pretty limited, as I’ve found most people opt to mind their own business. The influx of players at launch hasn't really changed the main emphasis on the E in the PvPvE structure of these runs, though human enemies can regularly play spoiler to otherwise solid runs if you’re not diligent.

There was a base level of tension with every step I took or round I let off.

It would be a mistake to confuse passivity with pacifism – this isn’t the Arc Raiders playerbase, which spent many of the opening months of that game more interested in helping than hurting. Whenever another player was in eye or earshot, it was assumed that a firefight was to follow, even though there is a specific truce ping that allows you the extend the olive branch to other players who you know are around but can’t (or don't want to) see in person. Like in all extraction shooters, the most tense PVP moment in DMZ happens when you’re waiting for your ride home and other players have decided that the extraction heli isn’t big enough for the both of you.

Hajin is a pretty lively place. Enemy patrols speed down roadways, sometimes as lone jeeps and other times in large caravans. Helicopters and drones streak across the sky looking for interlopers like me, or occasionally other NPCs from rival factions tearing the map apart. A train runs on a schedule, and you can absolutely hitch a ride on it to get from place to place. Squads of enemies could be posted up anywhere. Weird exploding robot dogs can sniff you out as you slink from building to building. There was always at least a base level of tension with every step I took or round I let off, because there was just enough going on around me that any push I made could be the one that knocked over the house of cards I was hiding behind.

Clashes with the NPC denizens of the DMZ start pretty tame, but ramp up as you and your squad make yourself more notorious by killing enemies and completing objectives. As enemy units start to don armor, pull up in more durable vehicles, and use higher grade weaponry to take you down, firefights can get desperate quickly. There's an instinct for people in pick-up groups to run out on their own, potentially to get first dibs on loot perhaps, and they almost always regret it – it doesn’t take much for enemies to get the drop on you, as you are only where you are and they are potentially anywhere and everywhere else.

Lieutenants are high-level boss challenges that really stand out as maybe the single most difficult thing you can attempt during any given run. They exist in specific regions, but where one in the forests lies in wait for unsuspecting operators to pass through their zone before springing their assault, another is dressed head to toe in armor with a mini-gun and will roam a piece of the city in hopes to stop you mid mission. They are better off avoided unless you’ve brought high-end gear with the specific purpose of hunting them.

The secret sauce of what makes the extraction shooter so appealing is the level of agency each player has to play how they want in a higher stakes PvP setting. You can go into every encounter guns blazing, or you can tip toe through them, taking the long way and hopefully having your patience and preparation rewarded. You can make racking up “objective completes” and bagging top tier loot your mission, or you can try to hunt down every human player you can. I like engaging in a bit of PvP, but my nearly 40-year-old eyes and reflexes frankly can’t keep up with the kind batshit action that goes on in the standard multiplayer modes (or even Warzone). In contrast, DMZ has created a great sandbox that serves all varieties of playstyles adequately.

There's always a way to jam up sightlines or to find some cover and take a breath. Every building has multiple points of entry, so sieges or chases through them always require some level of decision making. Sub-missions are always an option, where you can hack a tower for a UAV scan or SAM site coverage to support you in your main objective, but only if you’re willing the take on the added risk. I always felt like I could “yes, and” what was happening at any given moment, and thats not true for many of DMZ’s contemporaries.

Call of Duty’s top tier military shooter gunplay feels at home in DMZ.

Call of Duty’s top tier military shooter gunplay, toeing the line between the lethality of simulations and the snappy speed of arcadey romps, feels at home in DMZ as well. All of the new tweaks, like the way your weapons interact with your surroundings or movement adjustments, feel like they had roaming gunfights in an open world in mind. Being able to cancel out of mantles so you aren’t stuck in an animation when you least want to be feels like the kind of quality of life inclusion that Warzone demonstrated was needed for a long time. That said, the smattering of guns lack much in the way of real identities outside of the normal archetypal framings that have existed in gaming forever (shotgun, assault rifle, sniper, etc.).

You can also drop into story missions, which are quest chains that include more structure, resembling the campaign missions without the more cinematic story elements. These were pretty rowdy and unique, like an escort mission where our squad had to wheel an injured general out of a hospital, bed and all, while enduring swarms of enemy combatants. There's a finite number of these and the possible rewards are more limited, with less incentive to explore the places around you and no enemy players to pirate or evade. But they serve as a great tasting platter of the pace Dynamic Ops keep and a good tour of the various locations on the map you'll need to get familiar with.

In between missions, you’ll tend to all of your gear and progression from the safety of your FOB. Weapons, crafting ingredients, and consumables that you procure out in the field can be sorted through here, all of it put at risk when you equip it and redeploy. When you unlock the 3D printer and gunsmith areas, crafting new toys and upgrading weapons becomes the main way you spend your plunder to enhance your chances in Hajin. You can easily blow tens of thousands of won to trick out a weapon, but I found that the investments can be worth it in order to differentiate playstyles, like if you want a quieter load out with suppressors or a certain sort of optics to make mid-to-long range encounters more beneficial. I didn’t find the more money I invested automatically made my loadout feel more powerful, though.

Gaining experience is pretty easy as everything you do in the field gives you some amount of it. Progressing through the account level unlocks list was pretty breezy at first, helpful because a lot of recipes for some core equipment like grenades and ammo boxes are early rewards. At around half way up I found things slowed down quite significantly, but I also didn't find many late level goals to be enticing enough to turn up the grind. There's powerful kit, like a pistol that you can shoot downed teammates with to revive them, but I'm not torching all my spare time to sprint to level 64 to get it.

Similarly, Operators have individual sets of trait trees that you upgrade with points they earn in the field. These don’t appear to be novel to any individual fighter; there's no Ghost-specific ability where he gets unique buffs while saying something very British. Instead there are pools of passive traits across three categories, all operators have a choice between the same handful of major traits, and the 15 or so minor traits that fill out the trees are randomized. What makes an operator special seems to be limited mostly to how they look and sound, which was a bit of a bummer.

I was never champing at the bit to get my build “right.”

These traits can be meaningful in the field, though. One of the randomized traits I acquired for Valeria, Ammo Scavenger, doubled the ammo I got from ammo packs and made conserving my shots basically a thing of the past. The faster revive speed all operators have access to in their Survivalist tree has been the difference between clutch plays and total wipes on more than one occasion. There's definitely some kind of character building to be had here, but you really can’t know where it’s all leading until the next group of traits to choose from are revealed. And all that is before accounting for the fact that there's no way to reset or reallocate these trait points outside of abandoning the operator all together if you fail to extract with them (as opposed to spending some cash to get them back into the fight).

All of this created an environment where I was never champing at the bit to get my build “right.” Everything I picked worked at least a little bit. But I was also pretty detached from the whole process once I realized you really only had one shot at it per operator. I haven't maxed out any operator skill trees or account level reward path yet, but there are no substantial carrots left to tempt me down them either. Regardless of the trait investment, how good or bad I did in a run came down to how few bullets I ate in the process, and these trees, though valuable in specific cases, really didn't change that.

Sleeping Dogs Movie Director Explains Why The Adaptation Is Taking So Long

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Like many video game movies, Sleeping Dogs seems stuck in development hell. Even though it looked like progress on this adaptation of the cult-classic action game was once again picking up when Shang-Chi star Simu Liu started teasing new developments last year, and Timo Tjahjanto (Nobody 2) was later announced as the director, there's been little word since on whether the film will ever get off the ground. What's taking so long? According to Tjahjanto, the slow approach is by design.

"Trust me when I say Simu and I are doing our best to slowly make this come into fruition," Tjahjanto wrote on X/Twitter. "Trying to stay as loyal to Wei Shen's tale while adding layers to make the narrative more fitting into a feature film is not an easy task. We're taking our time to make the best version of [Sleeping Dogs] the movie."

Sleeping Dogs

Sleeping Dogs isn't exactly a complex story, as the 2012 game saw undercover cop Wei Shen's loyalties tested after he infiltrated a Hong Kong triad and found himself in the middle of a gangland civil war. It's a fairly standard tale for a game inspired by Hong Kong action movies, unless Tjahjanto is trying to weave in the DLC's story, which introduced an undead army and magical tea.

Sleeping Dogs is hardly the first video game adaptation to simmer in the background, as dozens of other projects are currently in varying stages of development. One notable example is the Ghost of Tsushima movie, which Chad Stahelski has been attached to direct for several years now; last we heard, Stahelski said a script was ready.

That was in 2023, and with the John Wick director currently working on finishing up the new Highlander movie starring Henry Cavill, don't expect to see Jin Sakai in the live-action flesh anytime soon--at least an anime adaptation of the popular samurai open-world game will land in 2028.
